Chemical Reactions and Stoichiometry: The Chemist's Arithmetic

A candle grows lighter as it burns, iron grows heavier as it rusts — does matter really appear out of nowhere or vanish into thin air? This chapter answers two questions: how to keep faithful accounts with equations (conservation of mass and balancing), and how to convert between invisible molecules and weighable grams (the mole and stoichiometry). This is the bread-and-butter skill behind every quantitative calculation in the branches that follow.

Introduction: Lavoisier's Balance

In the 1770s, Lavoisier ran an experiment so meticulous it bordered on obsessive: water was heated in a sealed vessel for 101 days, turning into "distilled water" and back again, weighed the whole way — the mass never budged by a hair. From this he established an iron law: a chemical reaction is nothing more than atoms rearranging; it neither creates mass out of nothing nor annihilates it. A candle becoming lighter is not matter disappearing — it turns into carbon dioxide and water vapor that escape into the air; iron becoming heavier as it rusts is the metal "taking in" oxygen from the air.

Think It Through: charcoal burns inside a sealed glass cover — how does the total mass of the cover and everything inside it change between before and after the reaction?

Hint: gases cannot get out, and nothing from outside can get in.

The total mass is exactly unchanged — the oxygen becomes carbon dioxide, the carbon becomes carbon dioxide; only the "way of combining" has changed. This is precisely the law of conservation of mass: in a closed system, the total mass is identical before and after a chemical reaction. Balancing chemical equations rests on it.

§1 The Chemical Equation: Faithful Bookkeeping

The chemical equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O reads "every 2 hydrogen molecules react with 1 oxygen molecule to form 2 water molecules". The numbers in front (the coefficient) give the ratio of particle counts, and at the same time the mole ratio — this is the key to the next section's calculations.

Three-step balancing: ① count every kind of atom on both sides → ② use the least common multiple to balance the most complicated molecule → ③ leave hydrogen and oxygen for last and double-check.

🔬 Hands-on: Balancing practice

📌 Coefficients are not decoration
The 2 : 1 : 2 in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O is the molecule ratio = mole ratio = volume ratio (for gases at the same temperature and pressure). Get one coefficient wrong and every calculation after it is wrong — balancing is the foundation of chemical arithmetic.

§2 The Mole: The Chemist's "Dozen"

A single water molecule weighs about 3×10⁻²³ g — a balance cannot weigh out "a few molecules", yet chemistry happens across billions upon billions of them. So chemists invented a gigantic counting unit: the molesymbol: mol.

🪜 Three-Level Explanation Ladder · What exactly is a mole?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Eggs are bought by the dozen (1 dozen = 12), paper by the ream (1 ream = 500 sheets), and molecules are counted by the mole (1 mol ≈ 6.02×10²³ of them). It is just a number — an entirely different thing from a mass unit like the gram.

LEVEL 2 · Definition and conversion

1 mol = 6.02×10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant NA). The remarkable part: a mass in grams whose numerical value equals the relative molecular mass M contains exactly NA molecules. So n = m / M (amount of substance = mass ÷ molar mass) builds a bridge between "grams" and "counts": 18 g of water ≈ 6.02×10²³ water molecules; 44 g of carbon dioxide ≈ 6.02×10²³ CO₂ molecules.

LEVEL 3 · Why 6.02×10²³?

This number was not chosen at random: 1/12 of the mass of a ¹²C atom defines the atomic mass unit u, and the number of atoms in 12 g of ¹²C is exactly NA. Because 1 u ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁴ g, the two are reciprocals — which is what makes the shortcut "M grams is, numerically, 1 mol" work. The link between u and g has been pinned down precisely by experiment (e.g., X-ray crystal-density methods, tracing back to the oil-drop experiment), and after the 2019 SI reform NA is a defined value.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · The mole's identity
✗
Misconception "1 mol of water is 18 grams, so the mole is a unit of mass" / "1 mol of iron weighs the same as 1 mol of water".
✓
Correct The mole is a counting unit: 1 mol of any substance contains 6.02×10²³ particles. 1 mol of water weighs 18 g, while 1 mol of iron weighs 56 g — same count, different mass (like a dozen ping-pong balls versus a dozen shot puts). The "18 g" is simply the molar mass M happening to make the numbers neat.

§3 Stoichiometric Proportion Calculations and the Limiting Reagent

Once the equation is balanced, the calculation is just converting by proportion. Take the laboratory preparation of hydrogen as an example: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑ — every 1 mol of zinc consumes 2 mol of hydrogen chloride and yields 1 mol of hydrogen.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: the full workflow in one pass

Example 1 · Full demonstration (watch me do it)
6.5 g of zinc is added to excess dilute hydrochloric acid. How many moles of hydrogen form at most, and how many grams of HCl are consumed? (Zn: 65; HCl: 36.5)
  1. Write the equation and balance it: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑.
  2. Convert the given quantity to moles: n(Zn) = 6.5 ÷ 65 = 0.1 mol.
  3. Convert along the coefficient ratios: Zn : HCl : H₂ = 1 : 2 : 1 → n(H₂) = 0.1 mol; n(HCl) = 0.2 mol.
  4. Convert the requested quantity back to mass: m(HCl) = 0.2 × 36.5 = 7.3 g.
  5. ✅ Answer: H₂ 0.1 mol (about 6.02×10²² molecules, roughly 2.24 L at STP), consuming 7.3 g of HCl. The routine in one line: grams → moles → convert by ratio → moles → grams.
Example 2 · Guided practice: the limiting reagent (do it with me)
3 mol of H₂ is ignited with 2 mol of O₂ (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O). Which reactant is left over? How many moles of water form at most?
  1. 💡 Approach: "convert" each reactant into the other to compare. If all the H₂ reacts, how much O₂ is needed? Coefficient ratio 2:1 → 3 mol H₂ needs 1.5 mol O₂.
  2. 💡 We actually have 2 mol O₂ > 1.5 mol → O₂ is in excess, and H₂ is the limiting reagent (the wooden-bucket effect: the shortest stave decides how much it holds).
  3. 💡 Compute the product from H₂: H₂ : H₂O = 2 : 2 = 1 : 1 → 3 mol H₂O forms, with 0.5 mol O₂ left over.
  4. ✅ Self-check: H used 3×2 = 6 atoms, O used 3; the source is 3 mol H₂ + 1.5 mol O₂ — conservation holds.
Think It Through: why is the hydrogen actually collected in the lab always less than the theoretical amount?

Hint: where might gas sneak off to? Does the reaction really go to completion without a single miss?

Three reasons: ① incomplete collection — a little hydrogen dissolves in the water or escapes; ② side reactions — the zinc contains impurities, or reacts with the acid in other ways; ③ reversibility and incomplete reaction. Hence the definition yield = actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100%; in industry this number directly determines profit, and it is one of the things analytical chemistry monitors (see Branch 03).

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • The law of conservation of mass is the basis for balancing: the number of each kind of atom must be equal on both sides.
  • Coefficients = molecule ratio = mole ratio (and, for gases at the same temperature and pressure, volume ratio as well).
  • The mole is a counting unit: n = m/M, N = n·NA (6.02×10²³).
  • The calculation routine: grams → moles → proportion by coefficients → moles → grams.
  • The limiting reagent determines the yield; yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100%.
