Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure: Why Atoms "Hold Hands"

Table salt shatters at a tap, an iron bar survives a thousand hammer blows, water boils only at 100 °C while hydrogen sulfide escapes at −60 °C — the answers to all these differences hide in one pair of questions: What holds atoms together, and what shape do they take once joined? This chapter works through the three fundamental chemical bonds, the rules that govern molecular shape, and the all-too-often underestimated intermolecular forces.

Introduction: The Same 10 Electrons, Worlds Apart

Water (H₂O) and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) — the neighbor living next door to hydrogen fluoride — are put together in almost exactly the same way: two hydrogens plus one nonmetal. Yet on Earth water is a liquid that keeps fish alive and falls as rain, while H₂S is a poison gas that boils away at −60 °C. The difference lies not in "which atoms are present" but in how the atoms are connected and how the molecules get along. That is the subject of this chapter.

Think It Through: Why do a sodium atom and a chlorine atom "combine" the moment they meet, while two neon atoms leave each other completely alone?

Hint: Recall the noble gases from Chapter 1 — how many electrons are in their outermost shell?

The outermost shell of a noble gas is already full (8 electrons; 2 for He): it neither lacks electrons nor has any to spare, so it has no drive to gain, lose, or share them. Na, by contrast, has just 1 "spare" electron in its outermost shell, while Cl is exactly 1 electron short of a full house — one gives, one takes, and both are satisfied. The essence of the chemical bond is the route atoms take toward a stable electron configuration.

§1 The Three Basic Chemical Bonds

The most useful tool for classifying a bond is electronegativity — an atom's "tug-of-war strength" in the contest for the shared electron pair. When the strengths are lopsided, one atom "steals" (ionic bond); when they are evenly matched, the atoms "share" (covalent bond).

Ionic bondAn outright "steal"Electrons transfer from one atom to another; the resulting cations and anions attract each otherΔEN ≳ 1.7NaCl, MgO, CaF₂

⚠️ Misconception Buster · Where ionic ends and covalent begins
✗
Misconception Ionic and covalent bonds are two mutually exclusive categories: in NaCl the electrons belong entirely to chlorine, while in H₂ they are shared perfectly equally.
✓
Correct Bond type is a continuous spectrum: the larger the electronegativity difference, the closer to the ideal ionic bond; only a difference of zero gives the ideal nonpolar covalent bond. NaCl sits very near the ionic end but still keeps a small sharing component; H–Cl is a polar covalent bond "leaning toward chlorine". The ΔEN criterion is only a rule of thumb, not an iron law.
📌 The metallic bond explains three hallmark properties of metals
Free electrons can move → electrical and thermal conduction; layers of ions can slide without falling apart → malleability (hammered into foil, drawn into wire); the electron sea absorbs and re-emits light → metallic luster. Covalent crystals (such as diamond) have no free electrons, which is why they are both brittle and non-conducting.

§2 Molecular Shape: The VSEPR Rules

A molecule is not the flat sketch of a structural formula — it is a three-dimensional object. The most effective entry-level tool for predicting shape is VSEPR theoryValence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion: the electron pairs around a central atom (bonding pairs + lone pairs) repel one another and settle as far apart as possible.

🔬 Hands-on: the molecular shape switcher

🪜 Three-Level Explanation Ladder · Why is water bent rather than linear?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Picture the oxygen atom as a person with two groups of companions: a hydrogen hanging on each arm (bonding pairs) and two bags of luggage strapped to the back (lone pairs). All four "take up room", so everyone spreads out at roughly 109° from one another — but the luggage, invisible though it is, is the bossier: it squeezes the two hydrogens closer together into a 104.5° V.

LEVEL 2 · VSEPR calculation

O has 6 valence electrons + 1 from each of 2 H → 8 valence electrons = 4 pairs. Two of them bond to the hydrogens and two are lone pairs. The ideal arrangement of 4 pairs is a tetrahedron (mutual angles of 109.5°). Repulsion ranking: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair; the two lone pairs press the two O–H bonding directions together, shrinking the bond angle to 104.5° and giving a V-shaped (bent) molecule.

LEVEL 3 · Hybridization language

In hybrid-orbital language: O's 2s and three 2p orbitals hybridize into 4 sp³ hybrid orbitals, which house the 2 lone pairs and 2 bonding pairs in an essentially tetrahedral orientation. The V shape is the skeleton left after "removing two vertices" from this electron geometry. The same logic yields NH₃ (one vertex removed → trigonal pyramid) and CH₄ (no vertex removed → regular tetrahedron). If the central atom has only 2 electron pairs (as C does in CO₂), the ideal arrangement is linear at 180°.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Predicting shapes with VSEPR

Example 1 · Full demonstration (watch me)
Determine the shape and bond angle of the CO₂ molecule.
  1. The central atom is C, with 4 valence electrons; the two O atoms each supply bonding electrons, giving 2 double bonds — under VSEPR, each double bond counts as one group of electron pairs (one "direction").
  2. The valence shell holds 2 electron directions, with no lone pairs. The best way for 2 groups to stay apart is opposite each other at 180°.
  3. ✅ Conclusion: linear, O=C=O, bond angle 180°. The polarities of the two C=O bonds point in exactly opposite directions and cancel → CO₂ is a nonpolar molecule (even though each bond is polar).
Example 2 · Guided practice (work along with me)
Determine the shape and approximate bond angle of the NH₃ molecule.
  1. 💡 N has 5 valence electrons + 1 from each of 3 H → how many pairs in total? — 4 pairs (3 bonding + 1 lone pair).
  2. 💡 The ideal geometry for 4 pairs is a tetrahedron. But one of them is a lone pair — will it widen or narrow the bond angle? — Lone pairs repel more strongly, narrowing it to about 107°.
  3. ✅ Conclusion: a tetrahedron with one vertex removed = trigonal pyramidal. The molecule as a whole is polar (the N end is negative).
Example 3 · Independent practice (your turn)
What is the shape of H₂S (S at the center)? Compared with H₂O, which has the larger bond angle? Commit to a conclusion first, then unfold to check it.
📝 Reveal the answer

S has 6 valence electrons → 4 pairs (2 bonding + 2 lone pairs) → bent (V-shaped), the same design as H₂O. But S is larger than O, its bonding pairs spread out more "comfortably", the squeezing effect of the lone pairs weakens, and the bond angle is slightly larger (about 92° for H₂S versus 104.5° for H₂O). Intriguingly, that is already close to 90° (bonding through pure p orbitals), a sign that S has little tendency to hybridize — the deeper-theory level always delivers an unexpected dose of honesty.

§3 Intermolecular Forces: The Invisible Hand Behind Boiling Points

Chemical bonds knit atoms into molecules; the attractions between one molecule and another are far weaker, and are collectively called intermolecular forces. They chiefly comprise van der Waals forces (ubiquitous, on the order of 1–10 kJ/mol) and the special hydrogen bond (10–40 kJ/mol — still far weaker than a chemical bond at 150–1000 kJ/mol).

The entry requirements for a hydrogen bond are strict: the H must be bonded directly to F, O, or N — three small, intensely electronegative atoms — so that H's nearly bare positive charge can strongly attract a lone pair on the F/O/N of a neighboring molecule and form an "X–H···Y" bridge.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · Chemical bonds ≠ intermolecular forces
✗
Misconception "The O–H bonds break when ice melts"; "boiling water destroys chemical bonds" — intermolecular forces are often misnamed "bonds" and lumped together with chemical bonds.
✓
Correct Melting/boiling/dissolving overcome intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces) while the molecules themselves remain intact — what rises off steam is still perfectly whole H₂O. Breaking an O–H covalent bond takes thousands of degrees or electrolysis. The energy scales differ by an order of magnitude: these are two different worlds.
Think It Through: Why does ice float on water? (For most substances, the solid is denser than the liquid)

Hint: the directionality of hydrogen bonds + tetrahedral coordination.

In ice, every water molecule forms a regular tetrahedral hydrogen-bond network with 4 neighbors — like an open, hollow cage frame that uses space wastefully. As it melts, some hydrogen bonds break, part of the network collapses, and molecules can "squeeze into" the gaps, so the density actually rises (reaching a maximum at about 4 °C). Ice floating on water lets aquatic life ride out the winter safely beneath the ice — one hydrogen bond saves a whole pond's ecosystem.

📌 Like dissolves like
Polar molecules (water, ethanol) mix readily — they can offer one another hydrogen bonds/polar attractions; nonpolar molecules (oils, gasoline) mix readily — they huddle together via van der Waals forces. And "oil and water don't mix" is simply the two sides ignoring each other. Nail polish (nonpolar) dissolves in acetone, sugar (many hydroxyl groups, polar) dissolves in water — it is all the same logic.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Three fundamental bonds: ionic (electron transfer), covalent (electron sharing — judge by the electronegativity difference), metallic (electron sea).
  • VSEPR: electron-pair repulsion sets the shape; lone pairs repel most strongly and squeeze bond angles smaller (CH₄ 109.5° → NH₃ 107° → H₂O 104.5°).
  • Molecular polarity = the vector sum of bond polarities; in CO₂ the symmetric bond dipoles cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar.
  • Intermolecular forces (van der Waals ≪ hydrogen bonds ≪ chemical bonds) set melting and boiling points and solubility; changes of state touch only the forces between molecules, never the molecules themselves.
  • Hydrogen bonding gives water its high boiling point, ice its buoyancy, and life its solvent.
