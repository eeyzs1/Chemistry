States of Matter and Solutions: The Social Life of Molecules

A single water molecule is colorless and formless; only when 10²³ of them gather do rain, fog, ice, and the oceans exist. This chapter watches two things: how temperature and pressure direct the particles' “way of living together” (the three states and the gas laws), and how the books are kept when substances dissolve in one another (concentration and dilution). Most chemical reactions happen in solution — this chapter is their backstage.

Introduction: One Cast of Actors, Three Stage Plays

Ice, water, and water vapor are made of exactly the same molecules; they differ only in the “style of the performance”: in ice, the molecules hold hands, each in its assigned place (a fixed formation); in water, they crowd together yet can still swap places (close but free); in water vapor, each molecule dashes off on its own (no interference). The director staging these scene changes is temperature and pressure. Why does a pressure cooker cook faster? Why does opening a soda go “psst”? You will find both answers in this chapter.

Think It Through: Why do wet clothes dry faster in summer than in winter?

Hint: evaporation = a water molecule's “jailbreak” from the liquid surface — and what does a jailbreak require?

Escaping the liquid surface takes enough kinetic energy. The higher the temperature, the greater the molecules' average kinetic energy, and the fraction of molecules “qualified to break out” rises exponentially (this foreshadows the Boltzmann distribution in Branch 04). That is why evaporation — and laundry — goes faster in summer, and why the higher temperature inside a pressure cooker makes reactions run faster.

📌 Temperature = a measure of the molecules' average kinetic energy
Temperature is not “how much heat” — it is a statistical measure of how violent the molecules' random motion is. This one viewpoint explains everything at once: faster evaporation, faster diffusion, faster reactions (kinetics in Branch 04), melting solids — in every case, the molecules are simply moving with more gusto.

§2 The Gas Laws: A Temper You Can Squeeze

Most of the space a gas fills in its container is “empty”, so gases are the easiest state to compress — and the easiest to describe quantitatively. Two classic rules (both established by quantitative experiments in the 17th–18th centuries):

  • Boyle's law: at constant temperature, pV = constant — squeeze the volume to half and the pressure doubles.
  • Charles's law: at constant pressure, V ∝ T (thermodynamic temperature) — raise the temperature and the volume expands.

Combine them into one all-purpose formula — the ideal gas law: pV = nRT (R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹; T must be the kelvin temperature, K = °C + 273.15).

🔬 Try it out: syringe compression experiment

Example 1 · Full demonstration: gas volume at standard conditions
At standard conditions (0 °C, 101.325 kPa), what volume does 2 mol of ideal gas occupy?
  1. List the knowns: n = 2 mol; T = 0 °C = 273.15 K (kelvin!); p = 101.325 kPa = 101325 Pa.
  2. Rearrange the ideal gas law: V = nRT / p.
  3. Substitute: V = 2 × 8.314 × 273.15 ÷ 101325 ≈ 0.0448 m³ = 44.8 L.
  4. ✅ Conclusion: at standard conditions, 1 mol of any ideal gas ≈ 22.4 L (the molar volume of a gas), regardless of which gas it is — because the volume is set only by the number of molecules and their kinetic energy, not by “who” they are.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · “0 °C is just 0”
✗
Misconception Entering the Celsius temperature directly in gas calculations: “T = 25 °C, so I'll put in 25”.
✓
Correct In the gas laws, T is the thermodynamic temperature (kelvin): T/K = t/°C + 273.15. Molecular kinetic energy at 0 °C is not zero — the true “zero kinetic energy” sits at −273.15 °C (absolute zero). Entering a Celsius temperature makes Charles's law come out completely wrong (the baseline of the V ∝ T proportionality is off from the start).

§3 Solutions and Concentration: The Ledger of Liquid-State Chemistry

A solution = solute (what gets dissolved) + solvent (what does the dissolving). In sugar water, sugar is the solute and water is the solvent. Three common “languages” describe how concentrated it is:

NotationDefinitionFormulaTypical setting
Molarity cMoles of solute per unit volume of solutionc = n / V (mol/L)Labels on laboratory reagent bottles
Mass fraction wSolute mass as a percentage of solution massw = m(solute) / m(solution) × 100%98% concentrated sulfuric acid, 75% disinfecting alcohol
ppm (parts per million)Extremely dilute concentrations; mg/L (approximately, for dilute aqueous solutions)1 ppm = 1 mg/LWater-quality and air-quality reports
Example 2 · Full demonstration: calculating amounts for preparing a solution
The lab needs to prepare 250 mL of a 0.1 mol/L NaCl solution. How many grams of sodium chloride should be weighed out? (M = 58.5)
  1. Align the units: V = 250 mL = 0.250 L.
  2. Find the moles of solute: n = cV = 0.1 × 0.250 = 0.025 mol.
  3. Convert to mass: m = nM = 0.025 × 58.5 = 1.46 g.
  4. ✅ Procedure: weigh out 1.46 g NaCl → dissolve it in a little water → transfer to a 250 mL volumetric flask and dilute to the mark. Note that the requirement is a final volume of 250 mL, not “add 250 mL of water”.
Example 3 · Guided practice: dilution (do it with me)
100 mL of 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid is diluted to 500 mL. What is the concentration after dilution?
  1. 💡 What stays the same before and after dilution? — the moles of solute (dilution adds no acid, only water).
  2. 💡 Write the conservation relation c₁V₁ = c₂V₂ → 2 × 100 = c₂ × 500.
  3. 💡 Solve: c₂ = 0.4 mol/L. The volume is now 5× larger, so the concentration is 1/5 of what it was.
  4. ✅ Self-check: n = 0.2 mol unchanged; 0.2 ÷ 0.5 = 0.4 ✓.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · Saturated ≠ concentrated
✗
Misconception“This solution is saturated, so it must be very concentrated.”
✓
Correct Saturated only means the solute has reached its dissolving limit at that temperature — and the limit itself may be high or low: saturated sugar water is cloyingly concentrated, while saturated calcium hydroxide solution (limewater) is dilute to below 0.02 mol/L. “Saturated” is a relative-solubility concept; “concentrated or dilute” is an absolute-concentration concept — two different things.
Think It Through: Why does opening a bottle of soda set off a rush of bubbles?

Hint: how much higher than atmospheric pressure is the pressure inside an unopened bottle? And how does gas solubility depend on pressure?

At bottling, carbon dioxide is pressed into the drink at 2–4 atmospheres — the greater the pressure, the greater the gas's solubility (Henry's law). The instant the cap opens, the pressure falls back to 1 atm, the solubility limit plunges, and the excess CO₂ must come out of solution — hence the eruption of bubbles. Shaking speeds up this release (more nucleation sites), which is why a shaken soda fizzes harder when opened — letting a soda settle before opening it is everyday chemical wisdom.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • The three states differ in how their molecules are arranged and how much order their motion has; temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy.
  • The two gas laws → the ideal gas law pV = nRT; T in kelvin; molar volume at standard conditions 22.4 L/mol.
  • Three languages of concentration: c = n/V (the lab), mass fraction (industry), ppm (trace levels in environmental work).
  • Dilution conserves the moles of solute: c₁V₁ = c₂V₂.
  • Saturated ≠ concentrated; gas solubility rises with pressure (Henry's law — the secret of fizzy drinks).
