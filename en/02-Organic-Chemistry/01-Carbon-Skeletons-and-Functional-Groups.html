02 Organic Chemistry · Chapter 1

Carbon Skeletons and Functional Groups: The Alphabet of Organic Chemistry

More than twenty million compounds are known, and the overwhelming majority of them contain carbon. What right does an element sitting in the sixth position of the periodic table have to shoulder such an enormous family? This chapter answers three questions: why carbon is special (tetravalency, strong bonds, chain- and ring-building), how the hydrocarbon family is classified (alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, arenes), plus the single most important sentence of organic chemistry — functional groups determine properties. At the end you will learn to count isomers and decode the simplest IUPAC names.

Introduction: Starting from a Food Label

Pick up any bottled drink and read the ingredient list: water, high-fructose corn syrup, citric acid, potassium sorbate, flavoring (ethyl acetate)… Apart from the water, nearly every item is a carbon-containing compound. Then look around: clothes (cellulose, polyester), tablets (aspirin), gasoline, perfume, dish soap, and even your own skin and hair — we live in a world framed from carbon skeletons.

In the early nineteenth century, chemists still believed that "organic compounds can only be made by living things" (vitalism), until 1828, when Wöhler synthesized urea — a molecule of living bodies — in a flask from the inorganic compound ammonium cyanate. From then on organic chemistry shed its mystical aura and became the science of carbon compounds. Note: "organic" here has nothing whatsoever to do with "organic food"; it is simply a name history left behind.

Think It Through: Could a "banana-flavored" drink and a natural banana owe their smell to the very same molecule?

Hint: Is a smell determined by the molecule itself, or by the molecule's "pedigree"?

Very likely the same one — isoamyl acetate. A banana's aroma is this very molecule, which the fruit itself synthesizes; the flavoring merely manufactures the identical molecule in a factory. Molecules carry no pedigree: same composition and structure, same substance. We will take this topic up again formally in Chapter 4's Misconception Buster.

§1 Why Carbon Is Special: Tetravalency, Strong Bonds, and Hybridization

Carbon's atomic number is 6: its electrons are arranged 2, 4, leaving 4 valence electrons in the outermost shell. It sits exactly 4 short of a "full octet" — no more, no less. So carbon almost always shares electron pairs with four atoms: this is tetravalency. Carbon neither readily gives up electrons (like sodium) nor readily grabs them (like chlorine); instead it shares them squarely, forming covalent bonds.

Tetravalency only answers "how many ports there are"; carbon's other gift is that its ports are exceptionally sturdy. The table below compares carbon with silicon, its neighbor in the same group:

BondBond energy (kJ/mol)Consequence
C–C≈ 347Strong and nonpolar → carbon chains and rings persist at ordinary temperatures
C–H≈ 413Even stronger than C–C → the "protective paint" on alkane skeletons
Si–Si≈ 226Weak → silicon chains spontaneously break beyond a few atoms
Si–O≈ 452Extremely strong → silicon prefers to build SiO₄ tetrahedra (quartz, rock)

Nature thus drafted two "architectural plans": silicon put all its talent into Si–O, building rock networks that are stable but monotonous; carbon used C–C chains and rings, together with "accessories" such as hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and the halogens, to build all the diversity from methane to DNA.

📌 The key idea in one sentence
Carbon = 4 ports + ports that are strong and durable. The carbon skeleton sets a molecule's "physique"; as you will see, functional groups set its "personality".

Hybridization: Three Intuitive Pictures

When carbon forms bonds, its atomic orbitals first "mix and rearrange" into several equivalent new orbitals, called hybrid orbitals. At the introductory stage, you only need to remember three pictures:

🪜 Three-Level Explanation Ladder · Why do most of the twenty million known compounds contain carbon?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Think of atoms as LEGO bricks: hydrogen has only 1 stud, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3 — you run out of combinations after two or three layers; the carbon brick has 4 studs, and carbon bricks can connect end-to-end and close into rings. Add a box of special accessories (O, N, halogens… these are the functional groups), and the number of buildable shapes naturally explodes exponentially.

LEVEL 2 · Data

Three conditions multiply together: ① tetravalency — carbon can connect to itself without limit, forming chains, rings, and networks; ② strong, nonpolar C–C bonds (347 kJ/mol) — long chains do not spontaneously break at room temperature and are not easily pulled apart by water; ③ isomerism — the same molecular formula can be arranged into different structures, and the isomer count skyrockets with carbon number: C4H10 2 isomers, C10H22 75, C20H42 over 360,000. Skeleton possibilities × functional group variety — that is how twenty million compounds pile up.

LEVEL 3 · The Essence

Carbon occupies the "golden mean" of Group ⅣA in the second period of the periodic table: elements to its left have too few valence electrons and tend to lose them, becoming metals; elements to its right have too many and tend to gain them, forming small molecules (O₂, N₂) or anions. Carbon sits in between, insisting only on covalent partnerships, and it forms stable covalent bonds with H, O, N, and the halogens alike. Even more crucially, carbon forms stable multiple bonds (C=C, C≡C) — its group-mate silicon, with diffuse 3p orbitals and poor π overlap, can hardly build a stable Si=Si; and with Si–Si weak as well, silicon's "complex-molecule chemistry" is a dead end, so it settles for stacking SiO₂ rock. The "silicon-based life" of science fiction, judged by bond energies, reads more like a philosophical thought experiment than a realistic proposal.

§2 The Hydrocarbon Family: "Pure Skeletons" of Carbon and Hydrogen Alone

Compounds made of only the two elements carbon and hydrogen are called hydrocarbons. Hydrocarbons are the skeleton layer of the organic world: according to whether the carbon skeleton contains multiple bonds, they divide into saturated hydrocarbons and unsaturated hydrocarbons; and then there is one more member with a built-in halo of stability — the arenes.

FamilyGeneral formulaStructural featureCarbon hybridizationRepresentativeEveryday link
Alkanes CnH2n+2 All single bonds (saturated) sp³ Methane CH₄ Natural gas, lighter butane
Alkenes CnH2n One C=C double bond sp² Ethene C₂H₄ Ripening fruit, plastic feedstock
Alkynes CnH2n−2 One C≡C triple bond sp Ethyne C₂H₂ Oxy-acetylene flame cuts steel plate
Arenes CnH2n−6 (benzene series) Contains a benzene ring (delocalized π) sp² Benzene C₆H₆ The parent of dyes and medicines

Notice the pattern in the general formulas: each extra double bond (or ring) costs the molecule 2 hydrogens. A "hydrogen shortage" is precisely the mark of unsaturation — as you will see in Chapter 4, the double bond is the stage on which all of an alkene's chemistry plays out.

Think It Through: Put a green banana and a few fully ripe apples into the same paper bag, and the banana ripens faster. Why?

Hint: What "broadcast" are the apples sending out? What molecule carries the signal?

Ripening fruit releases ethene (ethylene) — a small gaseous molecule with just two carbons and one C=C double bond. It is the plant's built-in "ripening trumpet": it diffuses into the banana, binds its receptor, and launches a whole program — starch hydrolyzing to sugars, pectin softening, and aroma ester synthesis. Fruit and vegetable logistics exploits exactly this: in transit, preservatives absorb ethylene to "extend shelf life"; once in store, ethephon (a reagent that releases ethylene) ripens the fruit on schedule. One small double-bonded molecule directs the timetable of the world's fruit.

§3 Functional Groups at a Glance: The Main Actors That Determine Properties

Picture the hydrocarbon skeleton as the concrete frame of a building: then the functional group is the key tenant who moves in — it is the group of atoms in a molecule that determines its chemical reactivity. Methanol CH3OH and methane CH4 differ by a single –OH, yet their properties are worlds apart: methanol mixes with water in all proportions and can be oxidized to formaldehyde, while methane lets almost nothing in. This is the first law of organic chemistry: functional groups determine properties.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · "Organic = natural and safe; inorganic = man-made and harmful"
✗
Misconception Organic substances come from nature, so they are gentle and harmless; inorganic ones are made in chemical plants, so they are dangerous. "No additives" and "all-natural" mean safe.
✓
Correct Safety depends on the molecule itself and the dose, not on its origin. A pair of counterexamples: ricin — an all-natural protein extracted from castor beans, thousands of times more toxic than potassium cyanide by mass (it was the weapon in the 1978 "umbrella assassination"); meanwhile synthetic vitamin C and natural vitamin C are the very same molecule (L-ascorbic acid), and your body cannot tell them apart in the slightest. Natural ≠ safe, synthetic ≠ harmful — this is the first sentence of any toxicology course, and the microscope that sees through marketing talk.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Isomerism's "Three Reagent Bottles"

Example 1 · Full Demonstration (Watch Me)
Three reagent bottles have lost their labels; their molecular formulas are: A C2H6O (ethanol), B C2H4O2 (acetic acid), C C2H6O (dimethyl ether). Identify the functional group in each, and predict: which bottle releases hydrogen with sodium metal? Which one turns purple litmus red?
  1. Identify the functional groups first: A, ethanol CH3CH2OH → hydroxyl –OH; B, acetic acid CH3COOH → carboxyl –COOH (a carbonyl and a hydroxyl fused into one); C, dimethyl ether CH3OCH3 → ether linkage –O–.
  2. The essence of the sodium reaction: sodium metal displaces "active hydrogen". A's –OH carries an active hydrogen, and B's –COOH an even more active one → A and B release hydrogen; C does not react (in an ether, every hydrogen sits on a carbon and is not active).
  3. Judging acidity: only the carboxyl group delivers an appreciable acid → only B turns litmus red, and it also releases carbon dioxide with sodium bicarbonate; A and C are both neutral.
  4. ✅ Conclusion + bonus: A and C share exactly the same molecular formula (C2H6O) yet are two different substances — their functional groups differ. This is a living example of the next section's "isomers": the molecular formula is the "class headcount"; the structure is the "seating chart".
Think It Through: Ethanol and water mix in all proportions, yet ethane is almost insoluble in water. Where does the difference lie?

Hint: What force holds water molecules together? Which extra "handshaking" group does ethanol have?

Water molecules pull on one another through hydrogen bonds. Ethanol's –OH can both donate and accept hydrogen bonds, so it slips seamlessly into water's "hydrogen-bond network"; ethane is a nonpolar pure hydrocarbon and cannot squeeze in — this is the microscopic explanation of "like dissolves like". Rule of thumb: small alcohols, carboxylic acids, and amines are water-friendly because they hydrogen-bond; the bigger a pure hydrocarbon skeleton, the more it shuns water.

§4 Isomers: Same Molecular Formula ≠ Same Molecule

An isomer is a compound with the same molecular formula as another, but a different structure. The simplest examples begin at 4 carbons: C4H10 has only two arrangements — the straight-and-stiff n-butane (the one in lighters) and the branched isobutane.

📌 The explosive growth of isomer counts
C4H10 2 isomers → C5H12 3 → C6H14 5 → C10H22 75 → C20H42 over 360,000. And that is counting nothing but pure alkanes! It is one of the sources of the "twenty million" arithmetic in the §1 ladder.
Think It Through: How many isomers does C₅H₁₂ have? Draw them on paper first, then check the answer.

Hint: Enumerate in a systematic order — "straight chain → shift one methyl → shift two methyls" — so nothing gets missed.

3: n-pentane (straight chain), isopentane (2-methylbutane, one branch), and neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane, two branches crowded onto the same carbon). Systematic enumeration — fix the length of the main chain first, then move the branch positions — is the basic skill for counting isomers; draw haphazardly and you will surely miss some.

§5 First Steps in Alkane Nomenclature: Issuing Every Molecule an ID Card

Common names are friendly but imprecise: what exactly are "alcohol", "wood spirit", and "chloroform"? Different countries — even different trades — keep their own sets of names. IUPAC nomenclature is the unified rulebook drawn up by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry — the molecule's "worldwide-valid address plate". The simplest version of alkane naming takes just four steps:

  1. Choose the longest carbon chain as the parent: chains of 1–10 carbons are named methane, ethane, propane, butane, pentane, hexane, heptane, octane, nonane, and decane (beyond that, number prefixes are used directly, as in undecane).
  2. Number the main chain: start from the end nearest a substituent so the substituent locants come out as small as possible (the "lowest locants" rule).
  3. Name the substituents: –CH₃ is methyl and –C₂H₅ is ethyl; the locant goes in front, as in "2-methyl".
  4. Assemble: substituents first, parent last; identical substituents are combined with di-/tri- counts (as in 2,2-dimethyl).
Example 2 · Guided Practice (Work with Me)
Name the structure CH3–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3. (Think through each step yourself before revealing it.)
  1. 💡 Find the longest chain: counting across gives 4 carbons (the second carbon also carries 1 branch carbon). Anything longer? Even counting the branch, the maximum is still 4 → the parent is butane. (Choose a 3-carbon main chain by mistake, and you conjure extra substituents out of thin air.)
  2. 💡 Numbering: counted from the left end, the branch lands at position 2; from the right end, at position 3. Since 2 < 3, the lowest-locant rule says number from the left end.
  3. 💡 Substituent: one –CH₃ → 2-methyl.
  4. ✅ Full name: 2-methylbutane (isopentane). Self-check: 4 carbons in the main chain + 1 branch carbon = 5 carbons, molecular formula C₅H₁₂ ✓ — the very same molecule as the "isopentane" in the §4 viewer.
⚠️ Common names are handy — but recognize the systematic names
"Alcohol" = ethanol; "wood spirit" = methanol (a few milliliters can cause blindness — the culprit behind counterfeit-liquor tragedies); "chloroform" = trichloromethane; "Formalin" = a solution of formaldehyde in water. Common names often carry history and hazard inside them — systematic names are the coordinates shared worldwide. When reading medicine and chemical labels, recognizing systematic names protects you better than memorizing common ones.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Carbon's tetravalency + strong, nonpolar C–C bonds let it build chains and rings and construct twenty million compounds; silicon lost out because Si–Si is weak.
  • Three pictures of hybridization: sp³ regular tetrahedron (109.5°), sp² planar (120°, double bond), sp linear (180°, triple bond).
  • Four hydrocarbon families: alkanes (saturated), alkenes (double bond), alkynes (triple bond), arenes (benzene ring); each extra double bond costs 2 hydrogens.
  • Functional groups determine properties: hydroxyl, aldehyde, keto carbonyl, carboxyl, ester, amino, halogen, and ether are the first faces to learn.
  • The same molecular formula can hide different structures (isomers); more branching means lower alkane boiling points; naming follows "longest chain + lowest locants".
