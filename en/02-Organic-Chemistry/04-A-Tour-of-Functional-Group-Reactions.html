A Tour of Functional Group Reactions: The Signature Moves of Six Families

Chapter 1 taught you to recognize the faces of the functional groups, and Chapter 3 taught you the language of mechanisms — now we put the two together. This chapter pays a house call on six families, one by one: alkenes (addition), arenes (substitution), alcohols (oxidation), aldehydes and ketones (nucleophilic addition), carboxylic acids and esters (esterification), and amines (salt formation), and every stop comes with a real-life link: margarine, breathalyzer testing, fruity esters, soap, and taming fishy odors. The finale is a clickable map of functional-group interconversions.

Introduction: Functional Groups in the Kitchen and the Medicine Cabinet

Open the kitchen: vegetable oil in the bottle (esters), acetic acid in the vinegar cruet (carboxyl group), ethanol in the cooking wine (hydroxyl group), the scent of banana (esters), the smell of fish (amines). Open the medicine cabinet: aspirin (carboxyl + ester groups), iodophor, vitamin C (an enediol). This chapter is like a matchmaking mixer: functional groups decide who may react with whom — the hydroxyl group meets an oxidizing agent, the carbonyl group meets a nucleophile, the amine meets an acid, and each pair starts its own story. Bring the curved arrows from Chapter 3, and let's step inside.

§1 Alkenes: Addition Opens Up the Double Bond

A double bond = 1 sturdy σ + 1 soft π. The π electron cloud is exposed on the surface of the molecule — a natural "electron blanket" that beckons to every electron-poor guest (H⁺, Br₂, H₂O ...). When a guest takes its seat, the π bond breaks open and its two atoms attach to the two ends of the former double bond — this is addition.

Real-life link #1: margarine. The long-chain fatty acids in vegetable oils carry several cis double bonds; the double bonds "kink" the molecules, so they cannot pack tightly and remain liquids. Hydrogenate them with hydrogen over a nickel catalyst: part of the double bonds get "ironed flat", the molecules straighten, snuggle together, and crystallize — and you have margarine (a solid). Push the hydrogenation too hard and trans fats form — modern processes control the degree of hydrogenation precisely.

CH2=CH2 + H2 —Ni, Δ→ CH3CH3

Real-life link #2: where plastic bags begin. Under a catalyst, ethylene molecules open their double bonds to one another and link hand in hand into long chains — addition polymerization:

n CH2=CH2 —catalyst→ –[CH2–CH2]–n (polyethylene)

The story of polyethylene (monomers, the plastics family, recycling) belongs to 06 Polymer Chemistry; here we merely plant a seed.

📌 Addition vs Substitution: One Sentence to Tell Them Apart
Addition is how an unsaturated bond "receives" a guest: the π bond opens, the guest attaches, the molecular skeleton never breaks and the carbon count never drops; substitution is a "roster swap": one group departs and another takes its place. Alkenes excel at the former; the benzene ring of the next section insists on doing only the latter.
Think It Through: Why is vegetable oil a liquid, while butter and lard are solids?

Hint: What shape do double bonds along a chain "fold" it into? Which packs more tightly, a straight chain or a bent one?

Vegetable oils are rich in unsaturated fatty acids: cis double bonds kink the chains, the molecules cannot nestle together, the melting point is low → liquid; animal fats are mostly saturated straight chains, packed like neatly bundled chopsticks, with strong dispersion forces and easy crystallization → solid. "Hydrogenating oil into margarine" is essentially ironing the double bonds flat and pulling the kinked chains straight — and the melting point rises accordingly.

§2 Arenes: A Choir That Refuses to Break Up

Benzene C6H6 is the most famous molecule in organic chemistry. In 1865 Kekulé proposed "a six-membered ring with alternating single and double bonds", yet even he was puzzled: if that were truly so, benzene should greedily undergo addition like an alkene, yet in reality benzene is remarkably aloof — addition is hard, substitution is easy. The truth is that benzene's 6 π electrons belong to no single edge but are delocalized above and below the entire ring, like a six-voice choir.

🔬 Try It Out: The Three Faces of Benzene

Benzene's signature reaction is nitration (an electrophilic substitution, catalyzed by concentrated sulfuric acid, giving nitrobenzene):

C6H6 + HNO3 —conc. H2SO4, Δ→ C6H5NO2 + H2O

Nitrobenzene can then be reduced to aniline (one of the protagonists of §6) — the dye industry's very first production line began with this reaction.

🪜 Three-Level Ladder · Why Does the Benzene Ring Find "Addition Hard, Substitution Easy"?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Picture the 6 π electrons as a choir that sings in perfect ensemble: addition would amount to pulling two members away to team up with outsiders, and the choir would disband on the spot — a loss of "team spirit"; substitution merely shows one H the door and lets a new member step in, and the choir sings on. The benzene ring would rather swap members than break up the choir.

LEVEL 2 · Data

"Team spirit" can be quantified as resonance energy: benzene's measured heat of hydrogenation is about 150 kJ/mol lower than expected for "cyclohexatriene" — and that energy is the dividend paid by delocalization. If addition occurred, the product (a cyclohexadiene derivative) would lose the delocalization, be badly disadvantaged energetically, and face a high barrier; substitution keeps (or ultimately restores) the aromatic delocalization the whole way, making it the economical route. That is why even when facing Br₂, benzene demands FeBr₃ catalysis and insists on substitution.

LEVEL 3 · Mechanism

The delocalized π system satisfies Hückel's rule (4n+2 π electrons; benzene has 6, with n = 1), forming a closed, stable shell. Electrophilic substitution, taking nitration as the example, proceeds in three steps: ① helped by concentrated sulfuric acid, nitric acid dispatches the electrophile NO2+; ② the electron blanket captures it, forming the σ complex (the Wheland intermediate, with the positive charge delocalized over the whole ring) — this step sacrifices part of the delocalization and is rate-determining; ③ H⁺ is expelled, and aromaticity is restored to full strength. The "intermediate" concept from Chapter 3 steps into reality here.

§3 Alcohols: The Ladder of Oxidation

In an alcohol the –OH is attached to a carbon, and by how many hydrogens remain on that carbon we distinguish primary alcohols (–CH₂OH), secondary alcohols (–CHOH–), and tertiary alcohols (–C(OH)–, no α-H). In the eyes of an oxidizing agent, the hydrogen on the α-carbon is the "door handle": only with a handle can the door be pulled open for oxidation.

  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid: CH3CH2OH → CH3CHO → CH3COOH (wine turning to vinegar as it sits is exactly these two steps, carried out with the help of airborne microbes).
  • Secondary alcohol → ketone (and it stops there): CH3CH(OH)CH3 → CH3COCH3 (acetone, nail-polish remover).
  • Tertiary alcohols: no α-H, so ordinary oxidants cannot pull the door open; strong oxidants simply cleave the chain.

Real-life link: the breathalyzer test. The classic breath-test tube is packed with silica gel soaked in acidified potassium dichromate: ethanol vapor in the breath reduces the orange-red Cr2O72− to green Cr3+ — the ethanol itself is oxidized. The depth of the color change is proportional to the amount of ethanol. Modern electrochemical sensors switched to the fuel-cell principle, but the chemical core — "ethanol gets oxidized" — has not changed.

⚠️ Methanol Is Not "a Slightly Stronger Liquor"
Industrial alcohol is often adulterated with methanol CH3OH. It walks the same "oxidation ladder", but its products are formaldehyde and formic acid — attacking the optic nerve and causing acidosis; a few milliliters can cause blindness. The same –OH, the same oxidation route, just one CH₂ apart — and the fates are utterly different: the culprit in counterfeit-liquor tragedies was never ethanol; it was methanol. A molecule's "ID card" tolerates not the slightest discrepancy.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Oxidation Products of Three Alcohols

Example 1 · Full Walk-Through (Watch Me)
Predict the products of the following three alcohols under a typical oxidizing agent (e.g. acidified potassium dichromate): A ethanol, B 2-propanol, C 2-methyl-2-propanol ((CH₃)₃COH).
  1. Classify first: check how many carbons are attached to — and how many hydrogens remain on — the carbon bearing the –OH. A: 1 carbon + 2 H → primary; B: 2 carbons + 1 H → secondary; C: 3 carbons + 0 H → tertiary.
  2. A climbs the ladder: the primary alcohol first oxidizes to acetaldehyde, and given sufficient conditions it oxidizes on to acetic acid (the orange-to-green change in the test tube is exactly this).
  3. B reaches the top in one step: the secondary alcohol oxidizes to acetone; a ketone has no α-H left to pull on, so the oxidation stops there.
  4. C has no handle to pull: with no hydrogen on the α-carbon, a tertiary alcohol does not react under ordinary conditions (no color change). ✅ A bonus identification method: dichromate distinguishes tertiary alcohols from primary/secondary ones.

§4 Aldehydes and Ketones: The Polarized Carbonyl

The carbonyl group C=O is a double bond between carbon and oxygen, but oxygen's electronegativity far exceeds carbon's, pulling the π electron cloud toward itself: the carbon carries a partial positive charge (δ+) and the oxygen a partial negative charge (δ−). So electron-rich nucleophiles (such as CN⁻, alcohols, and Grignard reagents) home in on that δ+ carbon — this is nucleophilic addition, the signature move of aldehydes and ketones (the mirror script of Chapter 3 §3, except that this time it is the C=O under attack).

The dividing line between aldehydes and ketones is one more step of oxidation: an aldehyde's carbonyl still carries an H and is easily oxidized onward to a carboxylic acid — which is why aldehydes are reducing; a ketone lacks that H and "declines the duel". The most classic test is the silver mirror test (Tollens' test):

CH3CHO + 2Ag(NH3)2+ + 3OH− → CH3COO− + 2Ag↓ + 4NH3 + 2H2O

The aldehyde reduces the Ag⁺ in Tollens' reagent to metallic silver, plating a mirror onto the clean inner wall of the glass — the silver film inside a thermos flask is deposited exactly this way. Glucose carries an aldehyde group (a reducing sugar), and early blood-glucose test strips used the very same principle (reducing Cu²⁺ to brick-red Cu₂O).

⚠️ Misconception Buster · "Natural and Synthetic Fragrances Are Different Substances"
✗
Misconception A fragrance extracted from roses and one synthesized in a factory are "essentially different"; the natural one must smell better and be safer.
✓
Correct Molecules care nothing about pedigree. As long as the composition and structure (including the stereochemistry) are the same, it is the same substance: identical melting point, boiling point, IR spectrum, and scent. The vanillin in vanilla beans and synthetic vanillin are the very same molecule (they differ only in trace impurities and price); perfumers can even synthesize fragrances not yet discovered in nature. Rather than asking "natural or synthetic", look at purity and dose — that is the language of toxicology (echoing ricin and vitamin C from Chapter 1).
Example 2 · Guided Practice (Do It with Me)
Two unlabeled reagent bottles: acetaldehyde CH3CHO and acetone CH3COCH3. Distinguish them by a single chemical method. (First ask "whose carbonyl still carries an H", then check step by step.)
  1. 💡 Find the difference: acetaldehyde's carbonyl carbon bears an –H (an aldehyde); in acetone both sides are –CH₃ (a ketone). Aldehydes can be oxidized; ketones resist it.
  2. 💡 Choose the reagent: Tollens' reagent (or freshly prepared copper(II) hydroxide). It exists precisely to "promote" aldehydes, which have reducing power.
  3. 💡 The observation: the one giving a silver mirror (or a brick-red Cu₂O precipitate) is acetaldehyde; the one showing nothing is acetone.
  4. ✅ Extension: hospital blood-glucose tests exploit the reducing power of glucose's aldehyde group — essentially the same "reducing-power exam". Note: fructose is a ketose, but under alkaline conditions it isomerizes to expose an aldehyde group, so it too is a reducing sugar — the details are left for 05 Biochemistry.

§5 Carboxylic Acids and Esters: Fruity Aromas and Soap

The carboxyl group –COOH donates protons readily (the electron-pulling carbonyl chimes in), so carboxylic acids are acid in more than name. Condensed with an alcohol under concentrated sulfuric acid catalysis with loss of water, it gives an ester — this is esterification (reversible):

CH3COOH + HOCH2CH3 ⇌ CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O (conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat)

Ethyl acetate smells of pineapple/nail-polish remover; isoamyl acetate is the standard "banana" flavoring — most fruit aromas are small-molecule esters. In the reverse direction, hydrolysis of an ester in base is called saponification: fat (a triglyceride of fatty acids) + NaOH → sodium salts of fatty acids (the main ingredient of soap) + glycerol. Saponification drags the reversible reaction all the way to the finish, because the carboxylic acid is instantly neutralized by the base and cannot flow back.

Think It Through: Why can soap wash grease away, while plain water never scrubs it off no matter how hard you rub?

Hint: In a molecule of sodium fatty-acid salt, what is one end like, and what is the other end like?

A soap molecule is "amphibious": the long hydrocarbon chain at one end is oil-loving (mingles with oil droplets), while the carboxylate end is water-loving (huddles with water). A few dozen soap molecules surround a small oil droplet, hydrocarbon chains tucked into the oil and carboxylate heads facing outward — forming a micelle. Once wrapped in micelles, the oil can be rinsed away with water; that is the whole secret of cleaning. A closing aside: pure hydrocarbon "oil" has no water-loving end, which is why plain water cannot wash it away.

§6 Amines: Small Basic Molecules

Remove one H from ammonia NH₃ and swap in a carbon chain, and you get an amine: methylamine CH₃NH₂, aniline C₆H₅NH₂ ... that lone pair on the nitrogen is the source of their personality — it can embrace a proton, so amines are basic and form salts with acids:

CH3NH2 + HCl → CH3NH3+Cl− (methylamine hydrochloride, a solid)

Real-life link: taming fishy odors. The smell of fish comes mainly from volatile trimethylamine (CH3)3N. When cooking fish, add vinegar or lemon juice: the acid "presses down" the amine into a non-volatile ammonium salt — the odor molecules can no longer fly into your nose, and the fish stops smelling "fishy". Chemistry explaining the culinary arts is often just this one line of equation.

A preview of the next branch: put an amino group and a carboxyl group into the same molecule, and you get an amino acid — the building block of proteins. How the 20 standard amino acids condense into peptide chains, and how enzymes work, is the opening act of 05 Biochemistry.

Think It Through: Why is aniline far less basic than methylamine? (Hint: that lone pair may be "busy elsewhere")

Hint: Could the delocalized π cloud of the benzene ring draw nitrogen's lone pair into joining the choir?

It could. The lone pair on aniline's nitrogen can delocalize into the benzene ring's π system (echoing the "choir" of §2), and a lone pair "borrowed away" has no hand to spare for grabbing a proton, so the basicity drops sharply. Methylamine has no such choir to join, so its lone pair waits wholeheartedly for a proton, and it is far more basic. The same functional group on a different skeleton changes personality — another fine example of "skeleton × functional group" jointly determining properties.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Alkene addition: hydrogenation makes margarine (ironing double bonds flat raises the melting point); addition polymerization makes polyethylene (a seed planted for 06).
  • The benzene ring, because its π electrons are delocalized (resonance energy about 150 kJ/mol), finds "addition hard, substitution easy", as in nitration.
  • Alcohol oxidation hinges on the α-H: primary → aldehyde → carboxylic acid, secondary → ketone, tertiary no reaction (the breathalyzer test; the methanol hazard).
  • The aldehyde/ketone carbonyl is polarized δ+/δ− and undergoes addition with nucleophiles; aldehydes are reducing (the silver mirror test), ketones are not.
  • Carboxylic acids and esters: esterification (conc. sulfuric acid catalysis, reversible) and saponification (NaOH, driven to completion); fruity aromas come from esters, soap from saponification.
  • Amines owe their basicity to the lone pair and form salts with acids to tame fishy odors; amino acids = amino group + carboxyl group in one molecule, the road to 05 Biochemistry.
