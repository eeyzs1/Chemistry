Introduction to Reaction Mechanisms: Curved Arrows and Three Classic Scripts

A chemical equation only tells you "where from, where to"; the reaction mechanism tells you "how it got there". This chapter first teaches you the chemist's secret language — the curved arrow (it depicts the movement of electron pairs) — and then introduces three classic scripts: free-radical chain substitution, nucleophilic substitution SN1/SN2, and electrophilic addition with Markovnikov's rule. Finally it untangles three high-frequency terms: activation energy, transition state, and intermediate.

Introduction: The Equation Is the Outcome, the Mechanism Is the Journey

Open a navigation app and it tells you "12 km in total" — that is the equation: point of departure and destination, both spelled out. But it does not tell you whether the route ran through a tunnel or up a winding mountain road, or at which service area you rested along the way. The reaction mechanism is precisely that complete driving route: where the electrons set out from, which intermediate stations they pass through, and how many energy-barrier "passes" they climb over.

Why is it worth learning? Because a mechanism lets you predict: will switching the solvent make the reaction faster? Will the product come out as a 50:50 mixture of left and right hands? Accident investigations (for example, why polymerization inhibitors are added to reaction vessels) depend on it entirely. The three scripts in this chapter cover most of the stage of organic reactions, and the admission ticket to all of them is a pen: knowing how to draw curved arrows.

§1 Curved Arrows: The Electrons' Line of March

Organic chemists record the migration of electrons with curved arrows. There are only three rules, and not one of them may be broken:

  1. The arrow starts where electrons are abundant: a lone pair, a π bond, a negative charge (–);
  2. The arrow points to where electrons are scarce: a positive charge (+), an electron-deficient atom, or a bond about to form;
  3. The arrow depicts the movement of "electron pairs", not of atoms, and certainly not of energy flow. A double-barbed arrow denotes a pair of electrons; a single-barbed (half) arrow denotes a single electron — the special notation of the free-radical script (see §2).
📌 Three Questions for Every Curved Arrow
Where does it leave from? (lone pair / π / negative charge) → Where does it go to? (positive charge / electron-deficient atom / new bond) → What is moving? (an electron pair, not a nucleus). Answer these three questions before drawing any mechanism, and your arrows cannot go wrong.

§2 Script One: Free-Radical Chain Substitution (Chlorination of Methane)

Mix methane with chlorine gas and keep them in the dark, and nothing happens; the moment sunlight strikes, they react explosively to give chloromethane and hydrogen chloride:

CH4 + Cl2 —hν→ CH3Cl + HCl (ΔH ≈ −104 kJ/mol, exothermic)

This "explodes-only-in-the-light" reaction follows the script of the free radical (an atom or group carrying a single unpaired electron, written ·), played out in three acts: initiation, propagation, termination. Click the three act buttons below to watch the whole stage:

🔬 Try It Out: The Three-Act Stage of a Chain Reaction

Taken together, the three acts explain a counterintuitive fact: a single photon can trigger thousands upon thousands of substitutions. Initiation happens only once, yet propagation can cycle 10⁴–10⁶ times, until some termination reaction breaks the chain. Industrial production of chloromethane and related solvents, and halogenation of alkanes in the laboratory, all follow this route.

Think It Through: Why is a little "inhibitor" added to commercially sold styrene monomer? What does it guard against during transport?

Hint: What happens when styrene's C=C meets a free radical? (Recall the polymerization to be covered in Branch 06.)

Styrene's vinyl group is very easily "attacked" by radicals, starting a chain polymerization: a single radical can set off an entire polymer chain, leaving a viscous lump at best, or at worst a runaway exothermic polymerization. Inhibitors (such as hydroquinone) are "radical scavengers": they preferentially combine with the occasional stray radical and destroy it at the initiation stage — in essence, a man-made "termination reaction". Distill the inhibitor off before use, and the polymerization can start cleanly.

§3 Script Two: Nucleophilic Substitution, SN1 and SN2

In an alkyl halide R–X, the carbon is slightly positive (δ+) because of the halogen's electron-withdrawing effect, so it attracts a nucleophile (Nu⁻, "nucleus-loving") bearing a lone pair or a negative charge, while the halogen departs in the role of the leaving group X⁻. For example: CH3Br + OH⁻ → CH3OH + Br⁻. Reactions of this "swap one group for another" type are called nucleophilic substitutions, and they come in two completely different routes:

FeatureSN2 (bimolecular nucleophilic substitution)SN1 (unimolecular nucleophilic substitution)
StepsOne concerted stepTwo steps: ionization first, then attack
IntermediateNone (only a transition state)Carbocation R⁺ (an energy valley)
Rate law v = k[RX][Nu⁻], both molecules count v = k[RX], depends only on the alkyl halide
Stereochemical outcome Backside attack → inversion of configuration ("umbrella flip") Planar R⁺ attacked from both faces → racemization
Substrate preferenceMethyl and primary halides (little steric hindrance)Tertiary halides (R⁺ more stable)

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Interrogating a Mechanism with Rate Data

Example 1 · Full Walk-Through (Watch Me)
A certain alkyl halide RX is hydrolyzed in aqueous solution. Experiment 1: doubling [RX] doubles the rate; experiment 2: doubling [OH⁻] leaves the rate unchanged. Does the reaction follow SN1 or SN2? What stereochemical outcome can you predict?
  1. Set up the yardsticks first: for SN2 the rate law is v = k[RX][Nu⁻] (the two molecules must collide to react); for SN1 it is v = k[RX] (the ionization step goes it alone).
  2. Experiment 1: [RX] ×2 → rate ×2, so the reaction is first order in RX. Both mechanisms are still compatible — nothing decided yet.
  3. Experiment 2: [OH⁻] ×2 → rate unchanged, so the nucleophile does not take part in the rate-determining step → rule out SN2.
  4. ✅ Conclusion: SN1 — the rate-determining step is the ionization of the C–X bond to a carbocation. Prediction: the substrate is most likely a tertiary halide (R⁺ is stable); the carbocation is planar, so OH⁻ attacks either face with equal probability → racemization (if that carbon is a stereocenter, the optical rotation will vanish).
📌 The Rate Law Is a Mechanism's Fingerprint
Change the concentrations and watch how the rate responds — this is the instrument chemists most often use to "interrogate" a mechanism. A bimolecular mechanism always leaves a second-order rate law behind; a unimolecular mechanism leaves only a first-order one. The "third interrogation" — isotopes and stereochemistry — supplies the other two instruments (see the ladder in §5).

§4 Script Three: Electrophilic Addition and Markovnikov's Rule

The π electron cloud of a double bond is like a blanket of electrons lying bare on the surface of the molecule, custom-made to attract electron-deficient electrophiles (E⁺, "electron-loving"). Take the addition of HBr to propene: in the first step the π electron blanket makes overtures to H⁺ (the curved arrow starts from the π bond), generating a carbocation; then Br⁻ attacks — an addition in two steps.

The crucial fork comes in the first step: to which end does the H add? The two possibilities give two different carbocations:

  • H to the terminal carbon → the positive charge lands on the middle carbon: a secondary carbocation (two C's send hyperconjugation "support");
  • H to the middle carbon → the positive charge lands on the terminal carbon: a primary carbocation (only one line of support).

The carbocation stability order is tertiary > secondary > primary > CH₃⁺ (alkyl groups are like passers-by holding umbrellas, spreading the positive charge through hyperconjugation and inductive effects). The system naturally takes the more stable route → the major product is 2-bromopropane. This is Markovnikov's rule: the hydrogen adds to the double-bond carbon that already bears more hydrogens.

CH3–CH=CH2 + HBr → CH3–CHBr–CH3 (major product)

Example 2 · Guided Practice (Do It with Me)
Predict the major product of the addition of HCl to CH3CH2CH=CH2 (1-butene), and justify your answer. (Walk through the electron flow on your own first, then check step by step.)
  1. 💡 Find the electron source: the π bond (electron-rich) → find the electron sink: H⁺ (the δ+ end of HCl). Draw the curved arrow from the π bond to H.
  2. 💡 Two possible additions: H to the terminal carbon C1 → positive charge on C2 (a secondary carbocation); H to C2 → positive charge on C1 (primary). Stability: secondary > primary → take the first route.
  3. 💡 Cl⁻ attacks the positive charge on C2 → major product CH3CH2CHClCH3 (2-chlorobutane).
  4. ✅ Double-check with Markovnikov's rule: "H adds to the carbon with more H's" — the terminal carbon C1 carries two H's, and indeed the H added there ✓. The two viewpoints (carbocation stability / the Markovnikov mnemonic) confirm one another.
Think It Through: Add a small amount of peroxide (ROOR) to the system, and the addition of HBr to propene suddenly goes "anti-Markovnikov". Why?

Hint: What do peroxides easily break into? (Recall Script One in §2.)

The mechanism switches scripts entirely: from the ionic carbocation route to the free-radical route. The first step becomes addition of Br· to the double bond — and it chooses the terminal carbon, so that the radical lands on the middle carbon (a secondary radical, more stable than a primary one); the radical then abstracts H from HBr to finish the job. Net effect: Br ends up on the terminal carbon and H on the middle carbon — precisely anti-Markovnikov. Note that this "peroxide effect" works almost exclusively for HBr (the bond energies of HCl and HI cannot cooperate with these two steps). One and the same net equation, two mechanisms, two product ratios — that is the theme of the next section's ladder.

§5 Activation Energy, Transition State, and Intermediate: Three Easily Confused Terms

Three characters on the energy profile, examined year after year — one sentence each to tell them apart:

  • activation energyEa: the energy needed to "climb" from the reactants up to the hilltop. The larger Ea, the fewer molecules get over, and the slower the reaction.
  • transition state‡: the maximum of the curve. A critical geometry with old bonds half-broken and new bonds half-formed; its lifetime is essentially zero, and it cannot be isolated or stored — it can only be glimpsed indirectly through spectroscopy.
  • intermediate: the valley between two peaks. Carbocations and radicals, for example — genuinely existing molecules with short chemical lifetimes (microseconds to milliseconds), but they can be caught by fast spectroscopy, and even kept alive a little longer at low temperature.

The rule for telling them apart: a maximum is not a species; only a minimum is. In a multi-step reaction, the steepest hill (the highest transition state) sets the overall rate — the rate-determining step. SN1's first hill (ionization) is its tollbooth.

🪜 Three-Level Ladder · Why Can Reactions with the Same Equation Have Completely Different Mechanisms?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Two villages sit on opposite sides of a mountain: village A (reactants) and village B (products). The equation says only "from A to B" — not whether you climbed the northern slope or detoured through the southern valley. Some travel on foot (one leg, straight through), some stop to rest at a service area along the way (an intermediate), and still others form relay teams (chains) — the villager roster and the destination are identical, yet the routes are worlds apart.

LEVEL 2 · Experiment

Chemists carry three "lie detectors": ① kinetics — how the rate depends on concentration directly exposes the participants in the rate-determining step (the SN1/SN2 verdict in Example 1); ② stereochemistry — SN2 inverts, SN1 racemizes; the configuration of the product takes the stand; ③ isotope labeling — give one atom a "traceable isotope ID" and see where in the product it ends up. Only with all three lines of evidence can a mechanism stand firm.

LEVEL 3 · Potential-Energy Surface

Unfold the positions of all the atoms in a molecule into a many-dimensional "potential-energy surface": reactants and products are two basins on that surface, and the paths connecting the two basins can be more than one, each path with its own sequence of transition states. Temperature, solvent, and concentration decide the "traffic volume" on each path — they can even switch the main road entirely, as when peroxides shift HBr from the ionic route to the radical one. A mechanism is therefore not philosophy but a falsifiable model: it always reserves the right to be overthrown by a new experiment. The quantitative tools (the Arrhenius equation, transition-state theory) await you in the 04 Physical Chemistry branch.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · "A balanced equation tells us how the reaction happens"
✗
Misconception Once the equation has been written out, the reaction steps must be self-evident; balancing the coefficients is the same as understanding the reaction.
✓
Correct The equation is a ledger of totals: it records only the initial and final states, never the path. The hydrolyses of methyl bromide and tert-butyl chloride have almost identical equations (RCl + H₂O → ROH + HCl), yet the former goes by SN2 (rate varies with OH⁻, configuration inverts) and the latter by SN1 (rate independent of OH⁻, racemization). A mechanism must be inferred from experiment — kinetics, stereochemistry, isotopes — and it is always the current best model, not an imperial edict. Balancing is bookkeeping; the mechanism is the detective work.
Think It Through: Raising the temperature gives the biggest boost to reactions with a large activation energy. Why?

Hint: What is it that temperature provides? If the climbers gain more stamina, which path benefits most?

Temperature raises the molecules' kinetic-energy reserves. For a reaction with a large Ea, "those able to make the climb" are vanishingly few to begin with (the far right tail of the Boltzmann distribution); raising the temperature multiplies the number in that tail — the boost is amplified exponentially. For a reaction with a small Ea, most molecules could already climb, so the room for improvement is limited. The quantitative relationship is described by the Arrhenius equation k = Ae−Ea/RT (covered in detail in Branch 04) — note that Ea appears in the exponent: the sensitivity is enormous.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Curved arrows are drawn from electron-rich sites (lone pair / π / negative charge) toward electron-poor ones; double-barbed = an electron pair, single-barbed = a single electron.
  • Free-radical chain substitution in three acts: initiation (photolysis gives Cl·) → propagation (H- or Cl-abstraction, radical regenerated) → termination (radicals combine).
  • SN2: one step, v = k[RX][Nu], inversion of configuration; SN1: two steps, v = k[RX], a carbocation intermediate, racemization.
  • Electrophilic addition goes through the more stable carbocation (tertiary > secondary > primary) → Markovnikov's rule; peroxides can flip HBr to the anti-Markovnikov script.
  • Transition state = the maximum (not isolable); intermediate = the valley (a real species); the highest hill sets the rate (the rate-determining step).
