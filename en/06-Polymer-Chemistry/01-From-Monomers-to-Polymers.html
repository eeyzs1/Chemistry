06 Polymer Chemistry · Chapter 1

From Monomers to Polymers: Small Molecules Link into Giant Chains

Ethylene is a small gaseous molecule, fleeting and formless; join hundreds of thousands of them head to tail and you get a plastic bucket that holds ten liters of water. This chapter makes one thing clear: when small molecules (monomers) link into giant chain molecules (polymers), material properties take a qualitative leap. We will take apart the two most important polymerization routes — addition and condensation polymerization — and see how chain architecture (linear? cross-linked?) determines the stiffness of plastics and the spring of rubbers, and why some plastics can be remelted again and again while others set once and for all.

Introduction: Three “Plastic” Friends

Tear open a bread bag: the thin film of cling wrap is so soft it barely holds a shape; unscrew a bottle of mineral water and the body is crisp, springing right back under a press; snap the black switch base of an old desk lamp and it is hard and brittle, cracking with a “crack” when it hits the floor — all of them are called “plastics”, yet their temperaments are worlds apart. All are synthetic organic macromolecules, so why are some soft, some tough, and some brittle?

The answer hides in the world of molecular chains. In the cling film, loosely entangled long chains can slide past one another; in the bottle, neatly folded, partially crystalline bundles of chains; in the switch base, the chains are welded together by chemical bonds into one huge three-dimensional net. To read all this, we must first answer a more basic question: how do small molecules “link up” into giant ones?

Think It Through: Both are “hydrocarbons” — why is paraffin oil (a mixture of small molecules) a free-flowing liquid, while polyethylene (long-chain molecules) is a sturdy solid?

Hint: Think about which is easier to move as a whole — a few loose strips of paper (short molecules) or an entire bound book (long chains).

The bigger the molecule, the larger the contact surface where chains “hug”, and the intermolecular dispersion forces (London forces) accumulate into something formidable — a single small molecule attracts its neighbors only weakly, so at room temperature it is a liquid or a gas; but a long chain with a molar mass in the tens of thousands offers hundreds or thousands of interaction sites with its neighbors, so the bulk can barely slide or volatilize, and it becomes a solid. The strength of a polymeric material comes, in essence, from “the strong covalent bonds inside each chain” plus “the summation of a great many weak interactions between chains”.

§1 What Is a Polymer: Molar Mass Is a Distribution

A monomer is the brick from which the edifice is built; a polymer consists of hundreds or thousands of monomer units repeatedly joined by covalent bonds — the literal sense of “poly-mer” is “many parts”. The number of repeat units in a chain is called the degree of polymerization (DP). Chains with a DP of 2 up to a few dozen are called oligomers; only in the thousands does a chain deserve the name of a true high polymer.

Each repeat unit of polyethylene, −CH2−CH2−, weighs 28 u (atomic mass units). For a chain with DP = 2000 the molar mass is about 56000; at DP = 20000, 560000. From small molecule to polymer, the chemical composition barely changes — all that changes is “how long the chain is” — a case of quantitative accumulation triggering a qualitative leap.

📌 Key Concept: Molar Mass Is a Distribution, Not a Single Value
Every water molecule is H2O with a molar mass of 18, always; but in a polymerization, when each chain “stops growing” is random, and the product mixes long and short chains. A polymer therefore has no single molar mass, only an “average molar mass” and a “molar-mass distribution”: the customary number-average molar mass M̄n (averaged over the count of molecules) and the weight-average molar mass M̄w (averaged with each molecule weighted by its mass); their ratio M̄w/M̄n ≥ 1 (the broader the distribution, the larger the ratio). Processing and properties are extremely sensitive to the distribution: many short chains mean easy flow but low strength; many long chains mean strength and toughness but hard processing.

By the way, one common impression deserves correcting: not all polymers are man-made. Cellulose (the framework of cotton and wood), starch, proteins, and natural rubber are all natural polymers; humans have been making synthetic polymers for barely a hundred years, while nature's “polymer factories” have been running for billions of years.

§2 Addition Polymerization: Open the Double Bond, Link into a Long Chain

Monomers that contain carbon–carbon double bonds (ethylene, vinyl chloride, styrene, propylene, and the like) can undergo addition polymerization: the π bond of the double bond opens, each monomer connects to its neighbors through the two freed “sockets”, and the monomer itself becomes one segment of the chain. The whole process loses no atoms whatsoever — the repeat unit has exactly the same formula as the monomer.

The classic industrial route is free-radical polymerization, which topples forward like dominoes, one ring pushing the next, in three steps:

  1. Initiation: the initiator (dibenzoyl peroxide, for example) decomposes on heating; one covalent bond splits homolytically, producing two free radicals R·, each carrying a single unpaired electron. That unpaired electron is fiercely reactive and “bites” onto any double bond it meets.
  2. Propagation: R· adds across the π bond of the monomer CH2=CH2; the monomer is incorporated at the chain end, and a brand-new radical reappears there — the activity passes along like a torch as the chain lengthens segment by segment. Thousands of monomers can be added each second; a complete chain typically forms within 0.1 to a few seconds.
  3. Termination: two active chain ends meet and the unpaired electrons pair up two by two (coupling termination), or one chain end plucks a hydrogen atom and hands it to the other (disproportionation termination); the radicals vanish and the chain stops growing.

The simplified equation for the polymerization of ethylene:

n CH2=CH2 → −[CH2−CH2]n−

Inside the brackets is the repeat unit, and n is the degree of polymerization. Replace one H with Cl and the monomer becomes vinyl chloride — the product is PVC; replace it with a benzene ring and you get polystyrene (foam plastic); replace it with CH3 and you get polypropylene. Different side groups, radically different material personalities — this is the polymer-world rerun of “functional groups determine properties” from branch 02.

§3 Condensation Polymerization: Link Step by Step, Squeeze Out a Small Molecule

The second route needs no double bond; it relies on functional groups that can condense with one another — carboxyl with hydroxyl to form an ester, carboxyl with amino to form an amide. In condensation polymerization every new link squeezes out a small molecule (usually water), which is why it is also called step-growth polymerization: two short chains of any length can join end to end, and chain length climbs step by step — a stark contrast to the “a finished chain in seconds” of radical polymerization.

Nylon 6,6: A Historic Scene

In 1935, Carothers at the DuPont company mixed hexamethylenediamine (an NH2 at both ends) with adipic acid (a COOH at both ends); the end groups condensed again and again into amide bonds −CO−NH−, giving poly(hexamethylene adipamide) — nylon 6,6:

n H2N(CH2)6NH2 + n HOOC(CH2)4COOH → −[NH(CH2)6NHCO(CH2)4CO]n− + 2n H2O

The amide groups can form vast numbers of hydrogen bonds that “lock” neighboring chains together — precisely why nylon fiber is so strong, and why it stormed the fashion world as stockings the moment it appeared in 1938.

PET: The Story of the Beverage Bottle

Terephthalic acid (two carboxyls) condenses with ethylene glycol (two hydroxyls), joined by ester bonds −COO− and squeezing out water molecules, to give poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET). It is transparent, tough, and an excellent gas barrier, the shared ancestor of the mineral-water bottle and of polyester fiber — one and the same polymer: blown into a bottle it is a container; drawn into fiber it is clothing.

🪜 Three-Level Ladder · Addition vs. Condensation — Where Exactly Do They Differ?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Addition polymerization is like coupling a train: once the locomotive (the initiator radical) leads, the carriages (monomers) hitch on automatically one by one, and a full train forms in seconds; condensation polymerization is like a handshake line: every person (monomer) has two hands (two functional groups), any two people can shake hands, the line lengthens step by step, and each handshake throws away a glove (a water molecule).

LEVEL 2 · Comparison Table

Monomer requirement: addition needs a carbon–carbon double bond; condensation needs two condensable functional groups (e.g. COOH+OH / COOH+NH2).
Byproduct: addition has no small-molecule byproduct and the repeat unit = the monomer; condensation generates a small molecule such as H2O at every step, and the repeat unit “slims down”.
Rate pattern: addition is a chain reaction — monomers are consumed in an instant and the chain length is fixed almost immediately; condensation is a stepwise reaction — the molar mass climbs slowly, and only high conversion plus an exact equimolar feed yield a high polymer.

LEVEL 3 · The Essence

The deep difference between chain and step growth lies in whether the active center persists: in free-radical polymerization, once a chain is initiated it races on carrying its active center — at less than 1% monomer conversion the chain is already near its final length, with monomer and polymer coexisting as two generations; in step-growth polymerization all molecules remain “equivalent” — any two end groups can react, and the average molar mass of the system rises steadily with conversion (the Carothers equation DPn = 1/(1−p); only at conversion p = 99% does DP reach 100). This also explains why condensation plants must keep drawing off the small-molecule byproduct — only then can the reaction be pushed toward higher molar mass.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · “A polymer is just plastic, and all plastics are the same”
✗
Misconception Plastic is one single thing, so all plastic bottles and bags can go into the same recycling bin.
✓
Correct “Plastic” is an umbrella name for hundreds of polymers. Read the recycling triangle on the bottom of the bottle: #1 PET (beverage bottles), #2 HDPE (detergent bottles), #3 PVC (pipes, raincoats), #4 LDPE (cling film, soft bags), #5 PP (microwave containers, straws), #6 PS (foam clamshells), #7 other (e.g. PC, PLA). Their chemical compositions, melting points, and processing methods are entirely different, melting a jumble of them together “contaminates” the batch and markedly lowers the quality of the recyclate — above all when PVC sneaks into the PET recycling stream, its chlorine discolors and embrittles the whole batch. Sorting is not a formality; it is a chemical requirement.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Identify the Polymerization Route and Write the Equation

Example 1 · Vinyl chloride CH2=CHCl (watch me do it)
Which polymerization route suits the monomer vinyl chloride, CH2=CHCl? Write the simplified equation and identify the repeat unit.
  1. Read the monomer's structure: the molecule has only a C=C double bond and a Cl side group, and no pair of condensable functional groups (no COOH/OH/NH2). → Rule out condensation polymerization.
  2. The double bond is a π-bond “socket” — the monomer can undergo addition polymerization, and no atom needs to be displaced.
  3. Write the equation: n CH2=CHCl → −[CH2−CHCl]n−. The double bond opens into a single bond, and the Cl stays attached at the side.
  4. ✅ The repeat unit −CH2−CHCl− has exactly the composition of the monomer (C2H3Cl), and there is no small-molecule byproduct — the hallmarks of addition polymerization, so the answer is self-consistent.
Example 2 · Terephthalic acid + ethylene glycol (do it with me)
The monomers terephthalic acid HOOC−C6H4−COOH and ethylene glycol HO−CH2CH2−OH are used to make PET. What kind of polymerization is this? Write the simplified equation and the byproduct.
  1. 💡 Read the functional groups: terephthalic acid has two COOH, ethylene glycol has two OH — “two-handed” molecules, able to condense.
  2. 💡 Two functional groups and no double bond → condensation polymerization, joined by esterification with ester bonds −COO−.
  3. 💡 Equation: n HOOC−C6H4−COOH + n HO−CH2CH2−OH → −[OOC−C6H4−COO−CH2CH2]n− + 2n H2O.
  4. ✅ Check: the repeat unit has “slimmed down” (by 2n waters) and a byproduct is formed — the signature of condensation, matching the task of making PET. Each condensation discards one H+OH pair; a unit built from two monomers loses 2 waters in total.

§4 Cross-Linking and Vulcanization: From Sticky Goo to Elasticity

Everything so far has been about linear chains — like so many strands of yarn. Raw rubber (natural rubber) is also a linear chain, but it suffers an awkward defect: sticky in summer, brittle in winter, and stretched lengths never return. The reason is that only weak intermolecular forces act between the chains, so under load they slide past one another and cannot spring back.

In 1839 the American Goodyear chanced to bring rubber, sulfur, and a hot stove together and discovered vulcanization: sulfur atoms throw covalent “bridges” (sulfur bridges) between neighboring chains, and the linear chains are stitched into a three-dimensional network — cross-linking. Once cross-linked, the rubber chains can no longer slide as a whole: pull, and the network is stretched open; release, and the network pulls everything back into shape — gooey raw rubber leaps to become a superbly elastic tire material.

The degree of cross-linking is a continuously adjustable knob: light cross-linking = soft and springy (rubber bands, tires); deep cross-linking = hard and brittle (hard rubber, Bakelite). Drag the slider and feel this knob at work:

📌 The Threefold Meaning of Cross-Linking
① Insoluble and infusible: no solvent can swell through the network, and heating cannot melt it open (it can only decompose); ② elastic recoil: under stress the chain segments are pulled straight, and on release the network snaps back on its own to the maximum-entropy coiled state; ③ tunable strength: cross-link density is the “volume knob” of hardness, twistable all the way from rubber band to hard rubber.
Think It Through: Why is lightly cross-linked rubber elastic, while over-cross-linked Bakelite is hard and brittle?

Hint: elasticity comes from “chain segments that can still stretch and coil”; brittleness comes from “no one can move at all”.

Elasticity needs two conditions: chain segments that are themselves soft and mobile (able to straighten when stretched and coil back when relaxed), and at the same time enough cross-link points to stop whole chains from sliding. Lightly cross-linked rubber has both, which is why a stretch can spring back. When cross-links are too dense, the free segments between neighboring cross-link points are too short to leave any room even to “straighten” — the whole block becomes one stiff mega-network that shatters under uneven stress: a hard, brittle thermoset material.

§5 Thermoplastic vs. Thermoset · A Quick Tour of the Material Families

With the key of “linear chains vs. cross-linked networks” in hand, an everyday question answers itself at once: what happens to a plastic when it is heated?

  • Thermoplastics: linear or branched architecture, with no covalent bridges between chains. On heating the chains gain kinetic energy and slide apart → the material melts and flows, then re-solidifies on cooling. It can be melted repeatedly and can be recycled and remade. PE, PP, PVC, PS, PET, and nylon all belong here.
  • Thermosets: the cross-linked network has already formed during processing (e.g. phenolic-resin Bakelite, epoxy resins, vulcanized rubber). On reheating the chains cannot slide; the material does not melt but only decomposes and chars as the temperature climbs → formed once, never remelted or remade. Electrical switch bases, pot handles, and circuit-board substrates are usually thermosets, chosen for exactly this heat-proof, shape-keeping quality.

⚠️ Chemical Safety Notes
Plastics have made life immensely easier, but keep in mind: ① never burn plastic in the open — burning PVC releases toxic HCl gas and organochlorine compounds, and the smoke from PS/PU is also irritant; ② plastic discarded into the environment barely degrades and fragments into microplastics that enter water sources and the food chain — please sort it for recycling; ③ foam PS catches fire very easily: keep it away from open flame; ④ for microwave heating, look for #5 PP containers — #1 PET and foamed tableware are not heat-resistant and should not be used at high temperature.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • A polymer is a giant molecule of monomers repeatedly joined by covalent bonds; molar mass is a distribution, described only by averages (M̄n, M̄w) and its width (ratio ≥ 1).
  • Addition polymerization: carbon–carbon double bonds open and interlink; a free-radical chain mechanism (initiation → propagation → termination), no byproduct, repeat unit = monomer (ethylene → PE, vinyl chloride → PVC).
  • Condensation polymerization: bifunctional monomers condense step by step, squeezing out a small molecule (water) at each step; high conversion is required (nylon 6,6 amide bonds, PET ester bonds).
  • Cross-linking / vulcanization: covalent bridges between chains (sulfur bridges) stitch linear chains into a network, turning sticky raw rubber into elastic rubber; the degree of cross-linking sets hardness.
  • Thermoplastic vs. thermoset: linear chains can be remelted and recycled; networks never melt, only decompose, and are formed once. Recycling codes 1–7 mark different families — always sort them.
