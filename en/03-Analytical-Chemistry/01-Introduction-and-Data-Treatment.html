03 Analytical Chemistry · Chapter 1

Introduction and Data Treatment: Learn to Distrust Your Numbers First

Blood glucose 5.2 mmol/L, sodium 120 mg/100 g, nitrate 3.2 mg/L — lab reports, food labels, and water-quality bulletins all keep broadcasting numbers at you. But these numbers — where do they come from, how many digits can you trust, and can they speak for the whole? This chapter is the foundation of all analytical chemistry: sorting out "what to measure" (qualitative vs. quantitative), "how well it was measured" (accuracy vs. precision), "where the error comes from" (systematic vs. random error), "how many digits to write" (significant figures), plus the two prerequisites of a good result — a calibration curve and representative sampling.

Introduction: It All Starts with a Lab Report

Blood drawn on an empty stomach in the early morning; by afternoon a report pops up on your phone: blood glucose 5.2 mmol/L (reference interval 3.9–6.1), uric acid 386 μmol/L, triglycerides 1.4 mmol/L. Three hours ago these were still part of what flowed through your blood vessels; now they have become a tidy row of numbers. Who turned matter into numbers? And, more to the point — on what grounds should you believe them?

The discipline that turns matter into "trustworthy numbers" is analytical chemistry. It asks only two questions: what is present (the clinical laboratory must confirm that it really is glucose, not some other sugar), and how much of it (that it is 5.2, not 5 or 6). And before pursuing "how much", the first skill an analytical chemist must master is finding fault with their own numbers — before any data are reported, they must clear four gates: Is it biased? Is it scattered? How many digits? Does the sample represent the whole? After this chapter, when you see "5.2 mmol/L" your brain will automatically supply the "± how much".

§1 Qualitative and Quantitative: First Ask "What Is There", Then "How Much"

qualitative analysis answers "what is there": which ions, molecules, or functional groups the sample contains. quantitative analysis answers "how much": the specific amount of a given component. These are not two courses to be examined one after the other, but two stations on the same workflow line — first confirm the target (qualitative), then determine its amount (quantitative); otherwise you may be measuring something entirely different from what you intended to measure.

Qualitative analysisQuantitative analysis
QuestionWhat is there? Is it present? Is it that substance?How much? What fraction? What concentration?
Typical settingsFlame test for sodium (yellow), iodine turning starch blue, preliminary breath screening for drunk drivingEvery single value on a lab report, food label, or water-quality bulletin
Form of the result"Detected / not detected", "it is Na⁺"5.2 mmol/L, 120 mg/100 g, 3.2 mg/L

The "units" of a quantitative result carry the order of magnitude of the content: water samples commonly use mg/L (roughly equal to ppm, one part per million), and trace pollutants may use μg/L (about ppb, one part per billion). Major components (>1%) and trace components (<0.01%) make utterly different demands on the method — measuring major components leans on "steadiness", while measuring traces leans on "sensitivity + not being drowned out by interference".

Think It Through: At a drunk-driving check, the police first have you blow into a screening breath tester, and only a positive result is followed by a blood sample sent for analysis. Which type of analysis does each step correspond to?

Hint: One answers "is it alcohol at all", the other answers "how many milligrams are in each 100 mL of blood".

The breath screen is qualitative (semiquantitative): a rapid on-the-spot decision about "is it present, is it ethanol", with the instrument's rough number serving only as backup; the blood test is quantitative: the law treats drivers in bands by blood alcohol content (e.g., ≥20 mg/100 mL counts as driving after drinking), so it must deliver an accurate, auditable value. Qualitative analysis goes first and narrows the field; quantitative analysis then delivers the verdict — this is precisely the internal division of labor within analytical chemistry.

§2 Accuracy and Precision: The Most Easily Confused Pair

accuracy is the closeness of a measured value to the true value — "is it biased"; precision is the closeness of repeated replicate measurements to one another — "is it scattered". The true value is unknowable (only a god's-eye view would have it); in practical work it is replaced by a conventional true value: the certified value on a reference-material certificate, or the mean of repeated measurements by several authoritative methods.

The classic analogy is target shooting: the bull's-eye is the true value, and every shot is one replicate determination. That gives four possible fates — accurate and precise (the ideal), accurate but imprecise (scattered yet centered on the bull's-eye), precise but inaccurate (clustered off-target), and neither accurate nor precise (scattered and off). 📌 The key idea in one sentence
Good precision is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for good accuracy: first ask "is it scattered" (random error), then "is it biased" (systematic error). A set of values that cluster tightly yet sit off-target as a group is the most deceptive kind of bad data.
🪜 Three-Level Ladder · Why can a "very precise" instrument give "very wrong" results?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

An electronic balance shows exactly 25.30 g every time it weighs the same object, down to the last digit — sounds perfect. But what if the reference mass inside it has worn down by 0.5 g? It will repeat the same wrong answer with flawless consistency. A steady readout only proves "the hand is not shaking", not that "the aim is true": this balance has been telling the same lie with scrupulous care all along.

LEVEL 2 · Data

Precision is governed by random error and measures how closely replicate results agree with one another; accuracy is governed by systematic error and random error together. Many displayed digits and a steady readout constrain only the random half. If an uncorrected systematic error is present — a drifting balance zero, a misgraduated volumetric flask, interference from coexisting components — every result shifts to the same side as a group, and the offset does not shrink with repeated measurement. Detecting it takes an external reference: reference materials, control experiments, spike recovery.

LEVEL 3 · Essence

Statistically, the standard deviation of the mean of n measurements shrinks as 1/√n — which is why random error can be beaten down by "doing it a few more times": averaging 4 runs halves it, averaging 100 runs leaves one tenth. But the systematic error δ is an offset carried identically by every measurement: E[x̄] = T + δ, and as n→∞ the mean converges to T + δ, not to the true value T. That is the mathematical root of "averaging cures scatter, not bias".

§3 The Error Family: Systematic, Random, and Gross Errors

Two Ways to Express Error: Absolute and Relative

absolute error: E = x − T (measured value − true value); it carries a unit and a sign, the minus sign meaning the result came out low. relative error: Er = E / T × 100%; it is unitless, which makes it easy to compare the "relative standing" across samples and methods. Shift by the same 2 mg: on a 2 g sample it is a 0.1% non-event, on a 0.02 g sample it is a 10% accident.

Example 2 · Guided Practice (Do It with Me): Calculating relative error
An analytical balance gives the mass of a sample as 2.0364 g, while a more reliable method assigns it the conventional true value 2.0512 g. Find the absolute error and the relative error, and get a feel for "why relative error is more useful". (A hint is hidden in each step — think it through yourself before expanding.)
  1. 💡 Absolute error: E = x − T = 2.0364 − 2.0512 = −0.0148 g. The minus sign says this weighing came out 0.0148 g low.
  2. 💡 Relative error: Er = (−0.0148 / 2.0512) × 100% ≈ −0.72%.
  3. 💡 Try another sample to feel its use: if the same 0.0148 g shortfall lands on a sample of only about 0.2000 g, then Er ≈ −7.4% — the absolute error is unchanged, but the smaller the sample, the more the relative error "hurts". So weigh small samples on a more accurate balance, or simply take a larger sample.
  4. ✅ Self-check: E has a unit (g) and a sign; Er is unitless and easy to compare across cases. A relative error of −0.72% is a normal level for major-component analysis.

Three Principal Sources of Error

systematic error keeps a constant direction or varies in a regular way across repeated measurements, and comes from the method, the instrument, or the operator; it can be tracked down and corrected. random error is unpredictable in sign and magnitude, arising from tiny fluctuations in the environment (temperature, air currents, readout jitter); no single measurement can remove it, but averaging many measurements suppresses it. gross error is an outright accident (wrong reagent added, scale misread, value transcribed wrongly) — the affected data must be rejected and the measurement redone, never papered over by "taking an average".

TypeDirectional behaviorTypical sourcesWhat to do

Systematic · instrumentConstantWorn weights, inaccurate burette/flask graduations, wavelength driftCalibrate the instrument, run a blank

Random error, though unpredictable, obeys a beautiful statistical law — the normal distribution: small errors are common and large ones rare, positive and negative are equally likely, and the mean of many measurements settles near the true value (provided no systematic error is meddling).

⚠️ Misconception Buster · Two identical readings mean the measurement is accurate?
✗
Misconception Two replicate readings that are exactly the same (e.g., 0.2000 g, 0.2000 g) show the measurement is both accurate and steady — the best possible outcome.
✓
Correct Exactly identical readings usually indicate precisely the opposite — insufficient instrument resolution: the real differences have been "clipped" into one displayed value; readings are far more often close than coincident. And "identical" speaks only to precision and says nothing about accuracy — the two readings could be biased together. Judge data by the spread of the deviations and by systematic error, not by "coincidental agreement".
Think It Through: A balance that was never zeroed (its zero point off by +0.0100 g) weighs the same sample 10 times in a row. Will averaging the 10 readings remove that +0.0100 g?

Hint: What do the errors of those 10 readings share in their direction?

No. The zero offset is a systematic error: all 10 readings carry the same +0.0100 g on their backs, and averaging leaves it exactly where it was (it does average the random jitter smaller). Removing it calls for zeroing/calibration, or a blank correction before weighing. In one sentence: averaging cures "scatter", not "bias".

§4 Significant Figures: How Many Digits to Write

significant figures are a datum's "uncertainty résumé": from the first nonzero digit through the last reliable digit, plus one estimated (uncertain) digit. The recording rule: copy down every digit the instrument reads out exactly, then add one estimated digit. A burette reading of 23.45 mL: the 23.4 comes straight from the graduations, and the final 5 is your estimate between two marks.

Three quick rules for counting digits:

  • All nonzero digits are significant; leading zeros on the left are not: 0.03040 → 4 significant figures (3, 0, 4, 0).
  • Trailing zeros after the decimal point are significant (they declare that the instrument resolved to that place): 0.20 → 2 significant figures.
  • Trailing zeros on a plain integer are ambiguous — state your case with scientific notation: 3.6×10³ is 2 figures, 3.600×10³ is 4.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · The instrument displays that many digits, so the result has that many significant figures?
✗
Misconception The calculator outputs 5.5209168…, so you copy down 5.5209168; the electronic balance shows 6 decimal places, so the result must be accurate to 0.00001 g — displayed digits = significant figures.
✓
Correct Significant figures are set by the weakest link in the whole measurement chain: instrument resolution, sample size, and method error jointly set the limit. Arithmetic only propagates uncertainty; it never creates accuracy — in 5.5209168 every digit past the fourth is "manufactured precision". Before reporting, ask: how many figures does the weakest number in the raw data have?

Rounding Rules: the Round-to-Even Rule (compact version)

What happens to the digits beyond the last retained place? Taking "keep 1 place after the decimal point" as the example:

  • 4.234 → 4.2: the first digit to be dropped is 3 < 5 — simply drop it.
  • 4.26 → 4.3: the first digit is 6 > 5 — round up.
  • 4.25 → 4.2: exactly 5 with no digits after it — the preceding digit 2 is even, so drop (keep it even).
  • 4.35 → 4.4: exactly 5 again — the preceding digit 3 is odd, so round up to make it even.
  • 4.2501 → 4.3: nonzero digits follow the 5, so it is not "exactly 5" — always round up.

Why does the 5 go to even? When large bodies of data are rounded, ordinary "round-half-up" always pushes a 5 upward and biases results systematically high; round-to-even gives "up" and "down" equal opportunity, making it statistically unbiased. And remember: round once, in a single step — never round in cascades: 4.549 goes directly to 4.5, not first to 4.55 and then to 4.6.

How Many Figures to Keep During Calculation

  • Addition and subtraction: align to the decimal places of the coarsest number (the one with the fewest decimal places).
  • Multiplication and division: keep as many figures as the fewest among the operands (counting total significant figures).
  • Exact constants such as stoichiometric coefficients and multipliers (e.g., 2, 1/2) impose no limit on digits; intermediate steps may carry one extra "guard digit", with rounding deferred to the final step.
Example 1 · Full Demonstration (Watch Me): Arithmetic with significant figures
Evaluate (1.276 × 4.17) + 0.20: with how many figures should the result be reported, and what is it?
  1. Size up each number: 1.276 → 4 significant figures; 4.17 → 3 significant figures; 0.20 → 2 significant figures (the trailing 0 is a declaration of instrument resolution, and 2 decimal places is where it is "coarsest").
  2. Multiply and divide first: multiplication and division keep to the fewest significant figures — 4.17 has only 3. 1.276 × 4.17 = 5.32092 → keep 3 figures → 5.32.
  3. Then add and subtract: 5.32 and 0.20 have the same number of decimal places (2 each), so add them aligned → 5.32 + 0.20 = 5.52.
  4. ✅ Conclusion: report 5.52 (3 significant figures). For comparison, the calculator chained through to 5.52092 — we have cut away the "manufactured precision": the extra digits are noise, not information. With many intermediate steps, keep 5.321 temporarily (one guard digit) and round to 5.52 at the very last step.

§5 Calibration Curves and Sampling: Two Prerequisites of a Good Result

The Calibration Curve: Trading the Known for the Unknown

An instrument never tells you concentration directly — a spectrophotometer gives absorbance, a chromatograph gives peak area, a balance gives mass. To translate "signal" into "concentration", you must first give the instrument a ruler: prepare a series of standard solutions of known concentration (e.g., 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 mg/L), measure each of their responses under the same conditions, and plot "signal versus concentration" to obtain a (nearly) straight line — this is the calibration curve. Then measure the unknown solution's signal and read its concentration back off the curve — this procedure is called the external standard method.

The intellectual core of the calibration curve is a single sentence: concentration is not read out — it is obtained by "trading the known for the unknown". Two practical points: the curve's concentration range must cover the unknown sample (extrapolation is dangerous); and instrument drift warps the ruler, so important work calls for calibration alongside the run and periodic replotting.

Sampling: The Representativeness of a Sample

The complete analytical chain is: sampling → sample preparation (crushing, mixing, dividing) → measurement → data treatment. Error propagates down the chain and is irreversible — a bias introduced upstream cannot be rescued by however precise an instrument sits downstream. A rule of thumb: sampling error is often larger than measurement error. One spoonful from a railcar of ore, one bottle from a river, one tablet from a batch of medicine — if the sample cannot stand for the population, the resulting number answers only for that one sample.

⚠️ Don't Lie at the Weakest Link
When the measurement error is 2% and the sampling error is 20%, the total error is dominated by sampling — the most expensive instrument will simply be measuring the wrong object with great precision. Design the sampling plan first (multiple points, strata, batches) so that the sample is qualified to represent the population; only then talk about measurement precision.
Think It Through: One bottle of water taken from the lower reaches of a river tests at nitrate 3.2 mg/L. Can it represent the whole river? Is the problem in the measurement or in the sampling?

Hint: Upstream versus downstream, bank versus mid-channel, before rain versus after rain — is the concentration identical everywhere?

Most likely not. A river's concentration varies with location, depth, season, and rainfall, and 500 mL of water is next to nothing beside a whole river. The problem lies not in the measurement (the instrument may be very accurate) but in the sampling — the sample failed to represent the "population". The right approach is a designed sampling plan: multiple points, multiple depths, simultaneous collection, combined into a composite sample when necessary. Remember: however accurate the data, they belong only to that one sample.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Analytical chemistry = qualitative (what is there) + quantitative (how much); workflow: sampling → sample preparation → qualitative → quantitative → data evaluation.
  • Accuracy asks "is it biased" (systematic + random error); precision asks "is it scattered" (random error); good precision is necessary but not sufficient for good accuracy.
  • Systematic error (method/instrument/operator) can be found and corrected; random error follows the normal distribution and is suppressed by averaging repeated measurements (the 1/√n rule); gross errors are rejected and remeasured.
  • Absolute error E = x − T; relative error Er = E/T × 100%, unitless and easy to compare.
  • Significant figures: decimal places govern addition/subtraction, figure counts govern multiplication/division; round with the round-to-even rule, in one step; displayed digits ≠ significant figures.
  • The calibration curve converts instrument signal into concentration (trading the known for the unknown); sampling error is often larger than measurement error — make the sample represent the population first.
