Titration and Classical Analysis: Measuring the Unknown with a Bottle of the Known

The white vinegar in your kitchen reads "total acidity ≥5.00 g/100 mL", the water-quality report reads "total hardness 250 mg/L", and the hydrogen peroxide at the pharmacy is labeled "3%" — most of these numbers come from the same classic procedure that takes three minutes to perform: titration. Take a solution of accurately known concentration, add it drop by drop to the solution being measured until the indicator suddenly changes color, then work backward from "how many milliliters were consumed" to "how much substance is present". This chapter thoroughly covers the idea of titration, jumps and indicators, standard solutions and primary standards, and the universal calculation framework c₁V₁ = n·c₂V₂, and along the way introduces redox titrations, EDTA hardness determination, and the venerable gravimetric method.

Introduction: Measuring the "Total Acidity" of Vinegar in Three Minutes

The white vinegar on the supermarket shelf has "total acidity ≥5.00 g/100 mL" printed on its label. How does a quality inspector verify it? Pipette 10.00 mL of the vinegar into a conical flask, add two drops of phenolphthalein, then let NaOH standard solution of known concentration run down from the burette drop by drop — slow down as the color approaches, and finally add half-drops at a time until the solution suddenly flushes pale pink and the color survives swirling. Read the burette: 16.xx mL consumed. Three minutes and one multiplication and division later, the total acidity is in hand.

The setup is cheap (one bottle, one burette, one conical flask) and reliable (relative error as low as 0.1–0.2%), and for more than a hundred years it has remained the workhorse for determining major components — it is the showcase of classical analysis: the stoichiometric relationship of a chemical reaction itself serves as the "calculator", with no expensive instruments required. This chapter answers four questions: How can a titration "count" out an amount of substance? Why does the indicator change color precisely within that one drop? Where does that bottle of "known concentration" come from? And how do you set up that universal calculation?

§1 The Idea of Titration: The Moment of Exactly Complete Reaction

The formal definition of titration: a standard solution of accurately known concentration is added to the solution being measured, until the two have reacted completely in exactly the stoichiometric ratio; the amount of the analyte is then back-calculated from the volume consumed and the known concentration. Its elegance lies in converting the measurement of an "amount of substance" into a measurement of "volume" — and volume is one of the quantities that can be read most accurately in a chemistry laboratory (a burette can be estimated to 0.01 mL).

Four key terms must be told apart once and for all:

TermMeaningNature
stoichiometric pointthe moment when the amount of standard added exactly matches the reaction stoichiometry of the analytea theoretical point — invisible and intangible
endpointthe point where the indicator changes color and you actually stopan experimentally observed point
indicatora "signal lamp" that changes color when a solution condition (pH, etc.) shifts abruptlythe visual stand-in for the endpoint
titration jumpthe region of abrupt pH (or potential, etc.) change around the stoichiometric pointthe "safety window" for judging the endpoint

The stoichiometric point and the endpoint can almost never coincide exactly; the bias their difference introduces is the endpoint error. The whole art of titrimetric methods comes down to one sentence: pin the endpoint inside the jump — as long as the indicator's transition range falls within the jump region, the endpoint error can be held to around 0.1%.

📌 The key idea in one sentence
A titration = using "known concentration × volume consumed" to count out "the amount of analyte". Identify the chemical reaction and its stoichiometric ratio first, and only then talk about readings and calculations — the balanced equation is the foundation of titration calculations, not decoration.
Think It Through: A burette reading error is about ±0.02 mL. Why do experienced operators arrange for the delivered volume to fall between 20–30 mL rather than 2 mL?

Hint: Revisit "absolute error vs relative error" from Chapter 1.

The reading error (initial plus final readings, about ±0.02 mL in total) is fixed: with 2 mL delivered the relative error reaches ±1%, while with 20 mL it is only ±0.1% — a tenfold difference. That is why the standard-solution concentration or the sample size is adjusted to place the delivered volume in a sensible range of the burette's capacity. This is the first field application of the "error mindset" from Chapter 1 to titration.

§2 Titration Curves, Jumps, and Indicators: What Happens Within a Single Drop

Take the most classic combination: 0.1000 mol/L NaOH titrating 20.00 mL of 0.1000 mol/L HCl. Plot the volume of NaOH added on the horizontal axis and the solution pH on the vertical axis, and you get the titration curve. For most of its length the curve strolls along as gently as a walk; only near the stoichiometric point does it "jump off a cliff" almost vertically:

V(NaOH)Solution statepH
19.98 mLstill 0.02 mL short (0.1% deficient)4.30
20.00 mLexactly complete reaction — the stoichiometric point7.00
20.02 mL0.02 mL in excess (0.1% excess)9.70

In other words: a difference of half a drop (about 0.04 mL) drives the pH from 4.30 up to 9.70 — the hydrogen ion concentration plummets by a factor of about 250,000. This steep slope is the jump. 🪜 Three-Level Ladder · Why does the indicator change color so abruptly "within that one drop"?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Picture a nearly balanced scale: the acid on the left pan is the "stock", and each drop of base you add is the "increment". While the stock is plentiful, every drop of base is instantly "eaten up" and the scale does not budge; but once the stock runs out, a single grain of sand can swing the pointer hard from one side to the other. The indicator is that pointer — it does not change gradually, it gets "punched through".

LEVEL 2 · Data

Before the stoichiometric point, nearly all the OH⁻ delivered is neutralized by H⁺, so the pH is governed by the residual H⁺ and changes only gently; near the point, the residual H⁺ has been consumed down to the 10⁻⁵ mol/L level, and the solution has lost its "buffer reserve"; after the point, even an extra half-drop of base leaves the OH⁻ with no opponent, and it simply accumulates in the solution — so between ±0.02 mL (0.1% short and 0.1% excess), the pH races from 4.30 straight up to 9.70. Phenolphthalein's transition range (8.2–10) falls exactly inside this window, which is why the color appears with a snap.

LEVEL 3 · Essence

The jump is not luck; it is written jointly by the equilibrium constants and concentrations of the system being titrated: the higher the concentration and the stronger the acid and base, the more lopsided the "stock vs increment" contrast on either side of the stoichiometric point, and the wider the jump; dilute the solution or weaken the analyte (small Ka), and the curve's slope eases while the jump narrows — it can even become too narrow for any indicator to fit inside. This is the reason behind "0.1 mol/L is the classic titration concentration" and "very weak acids cannot be titrated directly" (the quantitative criterion c·Ka ≥ 10⁻⁸ is left for advanced courses).

⚠️ Misconception Buster · The more indicator you add, the safer the endpoint?
✗
Misconception A few extra drops of phenolphthalein give a deeper, more eye-catching color that makes the change easier to see — so more indicator must be better.
✓
Correct An indicator is itself a weak organic acid/base and also consumes standard solution — the more you add, the larger the systematic error introduced; moreover, an overly deep color actually obscures the judgment, turning "spotting the change" into "guessing the color". One or two drops are enough for routine work. Choose an indicator by asking whether "transition range ⊂ jump range", and use as little as still shows clearly.
Think It Through: When acetic acid (a weak acid) is titrated with NaOH, is the pH at the stoichiometric point 7? Should you choose phenolphthalein or methyl orange?

Hint: What is in the flask at the stoichiometric point? Is a solution of sodium acetate acidic or basic after hydrolysis?

No, it is not 7. At the stoichiometric point all the acetic acid has become sodium acetate, and hydrolysis of Ac⁻ leaves the solution weakly basic (about pH 8.7 at the 0.1 mol/L level), with the whole jump sitting on the basic side (about 7.7–9.7). So you should choose phenolphthalein (8.2–10, inside the jump); methyl orange (3.1–4.4) would change color too early and cause a sizeable negative error. Indicator choice always follows the position of the jump, never personal preference.

§3 Standard Solutions, Primary Standards, and the Universal Calculation Framework

Where does that bottle of "known concentration" come from?

A standard solution arrives by one of two routes. Direct preparation: accurately weigh a quantity of a primary standard, dissolve it, transfer quantitatively, and dilute to the mark — the concentration is calculated and trustworthy at once. Standardization: prepare the solution at an approximate concentration first, then use a primary standard (or another standard solution of known concentration) to titrate its accurate concentration into place. Most standard solutions take the second route, because they cannot be prepared accurately: solid NaOH picks up moisture and absorbs CO₂; concentrated hydrochloric acid is volatile; KMnO₄ contains impurities and slowly decomposes — none of them can serve as a primary standard.

A substance must pass four tests to serve as a primary standard: high purity (≥99.9%), constant composition (strictly matching the chemical formula — crystal water included must be true to its name), stability (absorbing no water or CO₂, neither oxidizing nor decomposing), and a molar mass as large as possible (the same amount of substance then weighs more, reducing the relative weighing error). Common pairings: anhydrous Na₂CO₃ to standardize HCl, potassium hydrogen phthalate to standardize NaOH, and Na₂C₂O₄ to standardize KMnO₄.

⚠️ A standard solution is not a one-time deal
Left standing, a NaOH standard solution slowly absorbs CO₂ from the air and its concentration quietly drops. Re-standardize before important experiments, running several parallel standardizations (usually ≥3) and averaging only when the relative deviation is satisfactory (e.g., ≤0.2%) — the precision requirements of Chapter 1 land right here.

The universal calculation framework: c₁V₁ = n·c₂V₂

Let the titration reaction be aA + bB → products; at the stoichiometric point, n(A) : n(B) = a : b. Therefore (concentration in mol/L, volume in L):

c(A)·V(A) = (a/b)·c(B)·V(B) — the coefficient a/b is just the stoichiometric ratio

For a 1:1 strong acid–strong base pair it reduces to c₁V₁ = c₂V₂; HCl and Na₂CO₃ are 2:1; KMnO₄ and H₂O₂ are 2:5 — copy the ratio correctly from the equation first, then substitute the numbers: that is the entire secret of titration calculations.

Example 1 · Full Demonstration (Watch Me): Standardizing hydrochloric acid with sodium carbonate
A 0.1325 g sample of primary-standard anhydrous sodium carbonate (M = 106.0 g/mol) is weighed, dissolved in water, and titrated with the hydrochloric acid solution to be standardized, using methyl orange as indicator, to the endpoint (yellow to orange); 25.00 mL is consumed. Find the accurate concentration of the hydrochloric acid.
  1. Write the equation and copy the ratio: 2HCl + Na₂CO₃ → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑; the stoichiometric ratio is n(HCl) : n(Na₂CO₃) = 2 : 1.
  2. Count the primary standard: n(Na₂CO₃) = m / M = 0.1325 / 106.0 = 1.250 × 10⁻³ mol.
  3. Convert via the ratio: n(HCl) = 2 × 1.250 × 10⁻³ = 2.500 × 10⁻³ mol.
  4. Find the concentration: c(HCl) = n / V = 2.500 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.02500 L = 0.1000 mol/L. ✅ Significant-figure check: 4 digits in mass, 4 in volume → 4 in the result. In practice, 3 parallel standardizations are run and the average accepted only when the relative deviation is ≤0.2%.

§4 The Titration Family and Gravimetry: Not Just Acids and Bases

Titration, this "volume ruler", is not picky about reaction type: it works as long as the stoichiometric relationship is clear, the reaction goes to completion, the speed is fast, and there is a way to signal the endpoint. Four families are in common use:

FamilyStoichiometric bridgeTypical tasksEndpoint detection
Acid–base titrationNeutralization of H⁺ with OH⁻Total acidity of vinegar, caustic soda purity, nitrogen content of ammonium saltsAcid–base indicators such as phenolphthalein, methyl orange
Redox titrationElectrons transferredHydrogen peroxide, iron content, chemical oxygen demand (COD) of waterSelf-indication or redox indicators
Complexometric titration1:1 chelation of EDTA with metal ionsWater hardness (total Ca + Mg)Metallochromic indicators such as eriochrome black T
Precipitation titrationStoichiometry of precipitate formationChloride by argentometry (salt, water quality)Specific indicators such as potassium chromate

Redox titrations: permanganate's built-in signal lamp

In acidic solution KMnO₄ is a strong oxidant with a deep purple color, while its reduction product Mn²⁺ is nearly colorless. When it is used to titrate reducing species (H₂O₂, Fe²⁺, C₂O₄²⁻, ...) no separate indicator is needed: before the reductant is exhausted, every purple drop delivered is instantly "bleached"; the moment the reductant runs out, half a drop of excess MnO₄⁻ tints the whole flask pale pink — this is called a self-indicator, and a color that does not fade for about half a minute marks the endpoint (fading after long standing is slow consumption by reducing substances in the air and must not be misread). The reaction for determining H₂O₂ in acidic medium:

2MnO₄⁻ + 5H₂O₂ + 6H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5O₂↑ + 8H₂O

Note the stoichiometric ratio n(H₂O₂) = (5/2)·n(KMnO₄). KMnO₄ itself is not pure enough and its solutions are not stable, so it likewise cannot be prepared directly; it must be standardized against primary-standard sodium oxalate before use.

Example 2 · Guided Practice (Do It with Me): Determining the content of hydrogen peroxide
Pipette 10.00 mL of a medical hydrogen peroxide stock solution and dilute to the mark in a 250.0 mL volumetric flask; then pipette 25.00 mL into a conical flask, acidify with sulfuric acid, and titrate with 0.02000 mol/L KMnO₄ standard solution to a faint pink that persists for 30 s; 19.60 mL is consumed. M(H₂O₂) = 34.00 g/mol. Find the mass concentration (g/L) of H₂O₂ in the original solution. (Hints are hidden in each step — think for yourself before revealing them.)
  1. 💡 Titrant first: n(KMnO₄) = 0.02000 × 0.01960 = 3.920 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
  2. 💡 Apply the stoichiometric ratio to this 25.00 mL aliquot: n(H₂O₂) = (5/2) × 3.920 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.800 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
  3. 💡 Don't forget the dilution factor: the full 250.0 mL of dilute solution (= 10.00 mL of stock) contains 10 times this much H₂O₂ → 9.800 × 10⁻³ mol → c(H₂O₂) = 9.800 × 10⁻³ / 0.01000 = 0.9800 mol/L.
  4. ✅ Convert to mass concentration: ρ = c·M = 0.9800 × 34.00 = 33.32 g/L (about 3.3%, matching the medical hydrogen peroxide specification). Check: concentration and volume each carry 4 significant figures, so the result has 4; the dilution factor 10 is an exact number and does not limit the count.

EDTA complexometric titration: water hardness in one sentence

Water hardness (the total of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, reported as mg/L of CaCO₃) is measured by EDTA complexometric titration: EDTA chelates Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in exactly a 1 : 1 ratio, and eriochrome black T, which changes color once it is complexed, signals the endpoint — the calculation returns to the universal framework c(EDTA)·V(EDTA) = n(Ca²⁺+Mg²⁺), only with a ratio coefficient of 1.

Gravimetry: weighing the analyte out

The oldest and most laborious quantitative method — gravimetric analysis: add a precipitating agent so that the analyte is quantitatively converted into a sparingly soluble compound (e.g., SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄), then filter, wash, dry and ignite, cool and weigh, and back-calculate the content from the mass of the precipitate. It needs no solution of "known concentration" and trusts only the balance — slow but extremely accurate, and still the ballast of arbitration analysis and of value assignment for standard methods. Its weaknesses are just as obvious: a long procedure and low sensitivity make it suitable only for major components.

Think It Through: The endpoint of a permanganate titration is defined as "a faint pink that does not fade within 30 seconds". Why stress the 30 seconds? If the color fades after standing longer, does that mean the endpoint had not been reached?

Hint: Who is responsible for that trace of pink in the solution at the endpoint? Are there reducing substances in air and dust?

The faint pink comes from a slight excess of MnO₄⁻, showing that the reductant has been exhausted and the endpoint has just barely been passed — exactly the signal we want. But this thread of pink is not durable: reducing gases in the air, dust, and photodecomposition will slowly bleach it — hence the rule "no fading for 30 seconds": if the color holds over a short interval, the endpoint is declared reached; fading after standing for several minutes is background consumption, which does not count as "endpoint not yet reached", and must never be used as a reason to keep titrating.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • A titration = counting out the amount of analyte from known concentration × volume consumed; the hardware is only a burette, a pipette, and a conical flask — the first choice for macro analysis.
  • Four terms: stoichiometric point (theoretical point), endpoint (observed point), indicator (signal lamp), jump (the steep pH-change region around the stoichiometric point).
  • The jump comes from "stock exhausted, increment unopposed": between ±0.02 mL the pH can leap 4.30 → 9.70; an indicator's transition range must fall inside the jump.
  • Standard solutions come from direct preparation with a primary standard (pure, stable, constant composition, large molar mass) or from standardization; NaOH, HCl, and KMnO₄ must all be standardized.
  • The universal framework c₁V₁ = (a/b)·c₂V₂: copy the stoichiometric ratio correctly from the equation first, then substitute the numbers, and never forget the dilution factor.
  • The KMnO₄ method is self-indicating for H₂O₂/Fe²⁺; EDTA chelates 1:1 for water hardness; gravimetry precipitates and weighs — slow but extremely accurate.
