08 Environmental Chemistry · Chapter 1

Chemistry and the Environment: Giving the Earth a Chemical Checkup

The chemicals we release into the environment do not simply "vanish into thin air" — they keep migrating, reacting, and transforming in the air, water, and soil, then return to our dinner tables and our lungs in unexpected ways. This chapter tracks five major environmental issues through the lens of chemistry: the greenhouse effect, ozone-layer depletion, acid rain, water pollution, and green chemistry as the "antidote" — from mechanisms to remedies, we will see how chemistry is both the maker of these problems and their only solver.

Introduction: London's "Killer Smog"

In December 1952, a thick fog wrapped London for five full days. Every household burned coal for heat, and the SO₂ and particulate matter in the smoke were sealed over the city by a temperature inversion, unable to disperse. About 4,000 people died within five days, and deaths over the following months eventually totaled in the tens of thousands. This "killer smog" forced a fact upon all of humanity: chemicals discharged into the environment do not disappear — they keep reacting, and they find their way back to us.

environmental chemistry thus became an independent discipline: it studies the sources, transport, transformation, fate, and effects of chemical substances in the environment, and designs remediation and prevention strategies. It is a real-world "comprehensive exam" for inorganic, organic, and analytical chemistry.

📌 Environmental Chemistry's "Five Questions"
For any environmental issue, ask in turn: ① Where does it come from (source)? ② Where does it go (transport)? ③ What does it become (transformation)? ④ How concentrated is it (exposure)? ⑤ What does it do to whom (effects)? The greenhouse gases, CFCs, acid-rain precursors, and nutrients in this chapter all fit into this checklist.

§1 The Atmosphere: How Four Parts in Ten Thousand Move the Whole Planet

Start with the atmosphere's "ingredient list". Note the last column — what makes a molecule a greenhouse gas is not how abundant it is, but whether it has the ability to "grab infrared radiation":

ComponentVolume fractionAbsorbs surface infrared?
N₂78.08%No (transparent to IR)
O₂20.95%No (transparent to IR)
Ar0.93%No (monatomic, no vibrations)
CO₂about 0.042% (420 ppm)Yes (strong absorption band near 15 μm)
H₂O, CH₄, O₃, N₂O, etc.traceYes

The mechanism takes just two sentences: sunlight (mostly visible light) reaches the ground almost unimpeded and heats it; the ground then re-emits that heat outward as infrared radiation, which is absorbed by greenhouse-gas molecules such as CO₂ and re-emitted in all directions — roughly half of that energy heads back down to the ground. It is like tucking a blanket around the Earth: getting in is easy, getting out is hard.

🪜 Three-Step Ladder · CO₂ is only 4 parts in 10,000 — so what gives it the power to change the global climate?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Park a car in the summer sun with the windows up, and an hour later the interior can be 20 °C hotter than the outside: the glass lets visible light in but holds back most of the infrared. Greenhouse gases are that layer of "glass" for the atmosphere. An infrared photon has to cross tens of kilometers of air — even if the "interceptors" account for only a few parts per ten thousand, that is enough to shift the entire planet's energy budget, like a single drop of ink in a pool of clear water.

LEVEL 2 · Data

N₂, O₂, and Ar — 99.96% of the atmosphere — are completely transparent to infrared; the absorbers CO₂, H₂O, CH₄, and friends add up to less than 0.5%, yet their absorption bands happen to cover the energy-rich region of Earth's thermal radiation (about 4–50 μm, with CO₂'s strong absorption band near 15 μm) — not much, but "stationed right on the only road". Since the Industrial Revolution, CO₂ has risen from 280 ppm to about 420 ppm and the global mean temperature has climbed about 1.2 °C: a change of a few parts per ten thousand tips the heat balance of the whole planet.

LEVEL 3 · First Principles

Whether a molecule can absorb infrared depends on whether its dipole moment changes as it vibrates. N₂ and O₂ are homonuclear diatomic molecules: no vibration produces a dipole change, so they are infrared-"inert". CO₂ has no overall polarity, but its bending vibration and asymmetric stretch momentarily separate the centers of positive and negative charge, corresponding to the strong absorption near 15 μm. This is the quantum root of the rule that "greenhouse gases are mostly molecules of three or more atoms" — two faces of the same coin as the molecular vibration spectroscopy in Branch 04.

📌 The Greenhouse Effect Is Originally a Friend
Without the natural greenhouse effect, the mean surface temperature would be −18 °C; with it, Earth is a livable +15 °C. Today's trouble is not called "the greenhouse effect" — it is called the "enhanced greenhouse effect": the extra CO₂ and CH₄ humanity emits push the energy budget toward warming (per unit mass, CH₄ has about 28 times the 100-year warming effect of CO₂).
Think It Through: Water vapor is the most abundant greenhouse gas — so why is CO₂ the one sitting at the negotiating table?

Hint: Compare the two gases' "residence times" in the atmosphere, then ask "which one controls which".

Water vapor condenses and falls out within 8–9 days on average, and how much water vapor the atmosphere can hold is set automatically by temperature — warmer air holds more, and humans cannot control it directly over the long term. CO₂, by contrast, has a lifetime of a century or more and keeps accumulating after emission; it is the "knob" humanity has turned with its own hands. Warming → more atmospheric water vapor → a stronger greenhouse effect: water vapor is the "amplifier", while CO₂ is the "knob". That is why emissions-reduction negotiations keep their eyes on carbon.

§2 The Ozone Layer: One Chlorine Atom's Chain of Destruction

In the stratosphere, 15–35 km above the ground, oxygen is continuously "photolyzed" by high-energy ultraviolet light, producing a thin veil of ozone (compressed to one atmosphere, it would be only about 3 mm thick) — this is the classic Chapman mechanism:

O₂ + hν (λ<242 nm) → 2O O + O₂ → O₃

This ozone layer absorbs the most dangerous of the Sun's ultraviolet rays, UV-B, and is the "protective umbrella" that allowed life to colonize the land. Its destroyer is a story of almost perfect irony: in 1928 humans synthesized Freon (CFCs) to replace toxic, flammable ammonia and sulfur dioxide refrigerants — nonflammable, nontoxic, and chemically extremely stable, it was hailed as the "perfect chemical". Precisely that stability became the disaster: CFCs do not decompose in the troposphere, live long enough to diffuse all the way into the stratosphere, and only there are photolyzed by intense ultraviolet light, releasing chlorine atoms:

Cl· + O₃ → ClO· + O₂ ClO· + O → Cl· + O₂

With the two steps done, the chlorine atom is regenerated intact — this is a catalytic cycle: the net reaction is O₃ + O → 2O₂, and a single chlorine atom can tear apart roughly 100,000 ozone molecules. Worse still is Antarctica: on the surfaces of "polar stratospheric cloud" ice crystals at −80 °C during the polar night, chlorine reservoir molecules are converted into reactive Cl₂; each spring (September–November), the moment sunlight returns, the Cl₂ is photolyzed into chlorine atoms, and the alarming "ozone hole" appears over Antarctica in spring. In 1985 the British scientist Joe Farman identified it in observational data; in 1987 the Montreal Protocol was signed, phasing out CFCs — to this day the only environmental treaty to win genuinely universal ratification worldwide — and the ozone layer is expected to have largely recovered by the mid-to-late 21st century (around 2066).

📌 Same Molecule, Role Set by Location
Stratospheric O₃ absorbs UV-B and is the "protective umbrella"; tropospheric O₃, however, is a culprit of photochemical smog, irritating airways and damaging crops: NO₂ + hν → NO + O, O + O₂ → O₃ — the same photochemistry is a resupply line in the stratosphere and a pollutant over our cities. To judge whether a substance is "good" or "bad", always ask first: where, and how much.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · "The ozone layer has a hole in it, so that's why the globe is warming"?
✗
Misconception The ozone over Antarctica has a hole in it; UV leaks in through the hole and heats the Earth — the ozone hole and global warming are one and the same.
✓
Correct These are two independent chemical problems. The culprit behind ozone depletion is ozone-depleting substances such as CFCs, acting in the stratosphere, whose chief harm is more UV-B; the culprit behind warming is greenhouse gases such as CO₂ intercepting infrared radiation near the surface. In fact, ozone depletion actually cools the stratosphere. The only thing the two share: both stem from emissions of synthetic chemicals, and both prove that a global treaty can genuinely work.

§3 Acid Rain: Strong Acids Brewed Inside Cloud Droplets

Burning coal releases SO₂, while motor vehicles and high-temperature combustion release NOₓ. By themselves these are merely acidic gases; the real "brewing" happens inside cloud droplets: SO₂ is oxidized to SO₃ by O₃ and H₂O₂ within the cloud, and with water that immediately gives sulfuric acid, while NO₂ reacts directly with water to form nitric acid:

SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄ (in-cloud oxidation) 3NO₂ + H₂O → 2HNO₃ + NO

The most vivid damage is carved into marble statues — the pits etched by acid rain and the blinded faces:

CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂↑

📌 Why is the criterion pH < 5.6, not < 7?
The roughly 420 ppm of CO₂ in the atmosphere already dissolves into raindrops: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ (a weak acid), so even completely unpolluted rain has a pH of only about 5.6. Only a pH below 5.6 shows that "extra acid" — sulfuric or nitric — has been mixed in. Writing the criterion as pH<7 would misclassify every rainfall on Earth as acid rain — background levels are a concept environmental assessment can never sidestep.

Controlling acid rain relies on "fighting acid with base" and "escorting nitrogen back to N₂". Limestone–gypsum flue-gas desulfurization: a power plant sprays its flue gas with a limestone slurry, the SO₂ is absorbed and oxidized, and it finally becomes gypsum for building materials:

SO₂ + CaCO₃ + ½O₂ + 2H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O + CO₂

Selective catalytic reduction (SCR) for denitrification: over a catalyst, ammonia reduces NOₓ back to harmless nitrogen — 4NH₃ + 4NO + O₂ → 4N₂ + 6H₂O. After more than two decades of continuous desulfurization and denitrification, China's acid-rain-affected area has shrunk dramatically and cities with severe acid rain have essentially been eliminated; the flue gas cleanliness of "ultra-low emission" coal-fired plants now even exceeds that of many gas-fired plants.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Doing the Power Plant's Desulfurization Math

Example 1 · Full demonstration (watch me do it)
A coal-fired power plant burns 1000 t of coal per day, and the coal is 2% sulfur by mass.
① Without desulfurization, how many tonnes of SO₂ are emitted per day? ② With limestone–gypsum desulfurization (95% efficiency), how many tonnes of limestone (counted as CaCO₃) are needed per day at minimum?
  1. Write the combustion equation for sulfur: S + O₂ → SO₂. Sulfur entering the furnace per day = 1000 t × 2% = 20 t. The mass ratio of SO₂ to S = 64 : 32 = 2, so SO₂ = 20 × 2 = 40 t.
  2. Desulfurization efficiency 95% → SO₂ to be removed = 40 t × 95% = 38 t (the other 2 t is still emitted).
  3. Overall desulfurization reaction (simplified) SO₂ + CaCO₃ + ½O₂ + 2H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O + CO₂: SO₂ and CaCO₃ react in a molecular ratio of 1 : 1, so the mass ratio = 64 : 100.
  4. CaCO₃ = 38 × 100 ÷ 64 ≈ 59.4 t. ✅ Check: about 1.56 t of limestone per t of SO₂, matching the engineering rule of thumb (1.5–1.6); byproduct gypsum = 38 × 172 ÷ 64 ≈ 102 t, which can be made into plasterboard — a pollutant turned into a building material.

§4 The Hydrosphere: The Invisible War over Oxygen

To judge water quality, chemists first "measure the oxygen" of a water body. Three indicators, one table:

IndicatorWhat it measuresHow to read it
DO (dissolved oxygen)O₂ molecules in the waterHigher is better: saturation at 20 °C is about 9 mg/L; fish need ≥ 5
BOD₅ (5-day biochemical oxygen demand)Oxygen consumed by microbes decomposing organic matter over 5 daysHigher = more organic pollutants
COD (chemical oxygen demand)Oxygen consumed when potassium dichromate oxidizes organic matterFaster results than BOD, but it also "counts" hard-to-degrade substances

The bloodiest battlefield of this "oxygen war" is eutrophication: fertilizers and domestic sewage deliver large loads of N and P into lakes (phosphorus is often the limiting factor in freshwater — exactly why many countries have banned phosphate laundry detergents), and algae grow wildly into an "algal bloom"; when the algae die and settle, microbes decompose them, consuming large amounts of dissolved oxygen, and fish and shrimp suffocate — excess nutrients end in "oxygen depletion".

The "main force" of a municipal sewage treatment plant is also microorganisms: the activated sludge process blasts air into aeration tanks so that an army of bacteria feeding on organic matter "eats" the pollutants into CO₂ and H₂O; one step earlier, coagulation and sedimentation (Al³⁺ from alum hydrolyzes to form an Al(OH)₃ colloid that adsorbs suspended impurities) serves both drinking-water treatment and advanced treatment.

⚠️ Heavy Metals: Pollution That Cannot Be "Degraded"
Organic matter can be dismantled by microbes back into CO₂ and H₂O, but mercury, cadmium, lead, and chromium can only be moved, never destroyed. In Minamata Bay, microorganisms methylated Hg²⁺ into the fat-soluble CH₃Hg⁺, which biomagnified up the food chain until top predators carried concentrations on the order of a million times that of the water — the source of Japan's 1956 Minamata disease tragedy. Heavy-metal remediation relies on chemical "immobilization": adjusting pH to precipitate them as hydroxides, or locking them into extremely insoluble salts with phosphates or sulfides.

§5 Soil and Green Chemistry: From Cleaning Up to Preventing Pollution

Soil is the "silent one" among the environment's three spheres. Soil colloids (clay-mineral and humus particles) carry negative surface charges and can adsorb cations such as Ca²⁺, K⁺, and NH₄⁺ — this is the chemical essence of soil's nutrient retention, and also the handle for remediation: applying lime to raise the pH precipitates heavy metals, and adding phosphates locks them into insoluble minerals. Pesticides have evolved along the same lines: from DDT, "so stable it got everywhere", to today's readily degradable varieties designed around microbial degradation half-lives.

But however good end-of-pipe treatment becomes, it is still "mending the fold after the sheep are lost". In 1998, Anastas and Warner proposed the twelve principles of green chemistry, whose core is a single sentence: preventing pollution is better than treating it. The sharpest blade is atom economy (Trost, 1991) — getting as many atoms as possible from the starting materials into the product instead of into waste:

Atom economy = mass of the desired product ÷ mass of all starting materials × 100%

The classic case is the ibuprofen process: the old route took 6 steps with an atom utilization of about 40%; the green catalytic route takes 3 steps, at about 77%. Another everyday example is the three-way catalytic converter in car exhaust (Pt/Pd/Rh catalysts): 2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂, turning two pollutants into harmless substances at once — and lead "poisons" the catalyst, which is one of the chemical reasons leaded gasoline was phased out.

📌 Green Chemistry Is Not Chemistry's "Atonement"
It is chemistry's evolution: generate no waste that can be avoided (atom economy), use catalysis rather than stoichiometric reagents wherever possible, use water rather than organic solvents wherever possible, and design products to degrade safely after use. Green chemistry writes the environmental cost into the design stage of the reaction — chemistry's most thorough answer to the "London smog".
Think It Through: If a plastic bag is labeled "biodegradable", can we toss it away without a care?

Hint: What conditions does "degradation" require? Does the ocean provide them?

Most biodegradable plastics (such as polylactic acid, PLA) require industrial composting conditions — about 58 °C, high humidity, and specific microbial communities — to decompose within a few months. Dropped into the cold, oxygen-poor ocean, they persist for years all the same and fragment into microplastics that enter the food chain. Degradability only settles the "end-of-life destination"; reducing, reusing, and recycling (3R) still come first.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Environmental chemistry asks "source → transport → transformation → fate → effects" and designs remediation and prevention accordingly.
  • Greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases are transparent to visible light and absorb the ground's infrared; the natural greenhouse effect keeps the surface at 15 °C, and it is the "enhanced greenhouse effect" that drives warming; CO₂ is the knob, water vapor the amplifier.
  • CFCs release chlorine atoms in the stratosphere, and a catalytic cycle destroys ozone (1 Cl ≈ 100,000 O₃); the Montreal Protocol proves global treaties can work.
  • Acid rain = SO₂/NOₓ oxidized in clouds into sulfuric and nitric acids; the criterion is pH<5.6 (the CO₂ background); control relies on the limestone–gypsum process and SCR denitrification.
  • Eutrophication: excess N, P → algal blooms → decomposition consumes oxygen → fish suffocate (most dangerous at dawn); heavy metals do not degrade and biomagnify along the food chain.
  • Green chemistry prevents pollution at the source: atom economy, catalytic routes, safer solvents — preventing pollution is better than treating it.
