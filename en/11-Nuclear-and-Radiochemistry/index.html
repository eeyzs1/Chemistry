☢️ Nuclear and Radiochemistry: Transformations of the Atomic Nucleus

Ordinary chemistry tinkers with the electrons outside the nucleus — an atom that gains or loses electrons is still the same element. This branch steps inside the atomic nucleus: adding or removing protons and neutrons, the spontaneous decay of radioactivity, nuclear fission and nuclear fusion. Here, one element can become another (the alchemists' dream turns out to be real), one gram of matter can hold the energy of tons of explosives, and a scrap of bone can tell a story thirty thousand years old. We will also take a calm look at what radiation is, how much dose is dangerous, and how to protect yourself scientifically.

🎯 What you will learn in this branch

Nuclear chemistry studies changes in the atomic nucleus itself (decay, fission, fusion), while radiochemistry studies the behavior and applications of radionuclides (separation, labeling, tracing, protection). This chapter threads the two together on one guiding question — what happens to an unstable nucleus, and what can we do with it?

  • The discovery of radioactivity — Becquerel's drawer and the Curies' pitchblende: one of the most storied chapters in the history of science.
  • Decay types and conservation — what α, β, and γ rays are; writing balanced decay equations (mass number and charge equal on both sides).
  • Half-life and the decay law — why "half, and half again" never finishes yet stops being dangerous, and how carbon-14 dates ancient artifacts.
  • Fission and fusion — U-235's chain reaction and critical mass; how deuterium–tritium fusion lights the Sun.
  • Applications and protection — irradiated food, ¹⁸F-FDG PET imaging, radiotherapy; the time–distance–shielding triad and everyday dose facts (the banana equivalent dose).

After this chapter you will be able to read and write balanced nuclear equations, understand what the news actually means by "half-life," "critical mass," and "dose," and build a quantitative framework for the ionizing radiation in daily life (radon, cosmic rays, medical exams).

🗺️ Sub-branch map (signposts beyond this chapter)

  • Nuclear physical chemistry — nuclear binding energy and the map of nuclear stability (the chart of the nuclides), decay energy levels, and the nuclear shell model.
  • Radiochemistry — the separation, purification, and labeling chemistry of radionuclides: nuclide generators and isotope exchange.
  • Nuclear medicine — designing PET/SPECT imaging agents (such as ¹⁸F-FDG) and targeted radiotherapy drugs (lutetium-177, radium-223).
  • The nuclear fuel cycle — uranium refining, enrichment, fuel fabrication, spent fuel reprocessing, and geological disposal.
  • Radiometric dating — carbon-14, uranium–lead, and potassium–argon methods: the "nuclear clocks" that date Earth and the story of civilization.
  • Radiation chemistry and protection — interactions of ionizing radiation with matter, dosimetry, shielding design, and environmental monitoring.
