Transition Metals and Coordination Chemistry: The Colorful Belt in the Middle

The red of ruby is chromium, and so is the green of emerald; blue copper sulfate, yellow rust, purple permanganate, and the hemoglobin that ferries oxygen through your veins — the broad d-block belt across the middle of the periodic table supplies most of nature's colors and the key reactions of life. This chapter first looks at the three hallmarks of the d-block metals (multiple oxidation states, colored compounds, ready catalysis), then teaches a brand-new language — the coordination complex: central ion, ligands, coordination number. Finally, the "d-orbital splitting" of crystal field theory lets you see in one step why a colorless ion takes on vivid color the moment it meets a ligand.

Introduction: Half the Shelves in a Paint Shop

Step into a paint shop: ultramarine, cobalt blue, chromium green, cadmium red, iron yellow... Count the elements named on the labels — most come from columns 3–12 in the middle of the periodic table. The elements in this "belt" are called transition metals — "transition" refers to how their properties bridge the reactive s-block metals and the p-block elements, and to the fact that their electrons are filling the d orbitals of the second-outermost shell one by one (recall the reflection question in Chapter 1 §1: 4s fills before 3d, so d electrons "live in an inner shell yet join the lineup partway through").

These "inner-shell" d electrons are precisely what give transition metals three superpowers: multiple oxidation states, vivid colors, and outstanding catalytic ability. To truly read the colors, though, we must first learn a new "molecular grammar" — coordination chemistry. Its object of study, the coordination complex, is shaped like a nesting doll: a metal ion sits at the center while a ring of molecules or ions joins hands around it. Do not underestimate this structure — heme, chlorophyll, and vitamin B12 are all complexes.

§1 Three Hallmarks of the d Block: Variable Oxidation States, Color, and Catalysis

Hallmark one: multiple oxidation states. Main-group metals usually have just one "respectable" oxidation state (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺), yet transition metals change oxidation states as naturally as changing clothes: iron has Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, copper has Cu⁺/Cu²⁺, and manganese runs all the way from +2 to +7 (the Mn in KMnO4 is +7). The reason is that same set of d electrons: the second-outermost d subshell and the outermost s subshell are close in energy, so electrons from both shells can join the fight — giving up a few more or a few less does no real damage.

Hallmark two: colored compounds. d-block solutions are the most photogenic part of any chemistry lab — here is a table of the regulars:

Hallmark three: catalysis. Variable oxidation states let a transition metal "accept first, deliver later": an iron catalyst helps N2 and H2 build ammonia step by step (the Haber process, which feeds half the world's population); V2O5 shuttles back and forth between oxidation states in the contact process for sulfuric acid; the Pt and Pd in car-exhaust cleanup convert CO and NO into harmless gases; and the active center of nearly every enzyme in your body is occupied by a transition metal. Reaching out a d orbital to give the reactants a pull, then withdrawing intact — that is a catalyst's gift.

Think It Through: Why do main-group metals rarely show multiple oxidation states, while transition metals often have five or six?

Hint: Recall Chapter 1 — after Na loses its one outermost electron, what structure is left? Is it easy to break into that?

After losing its 3s electron, Na has the closed-shell structure of Ne; touching an electron in the second shell would raise the ionization energy by an order of magnitude — so main-group elements are "one oxidation state for life". Transition metals are different: they lose the ns electrons first in reactions, and the remaining (n−1)d electrons are close to one another in energy and can be lost one at a time — losing 2, 3, ... the cost climbs a "gentle slope" rather than a "cliff". More oxidation states = more redox roles = capital for catalysis; the three hallmarks are really three faces of one fact (the d electrons sit at flexible energies).

§2 The Composition, Structure, and Naming of Complexes

Add ammonia dropwise to a copper(II) sulfate solution and, beyond the color change, something more fundamental happens: the ammonia molecules use the lone pair on their nitrogen atoms to grab Cu²⁺, forming a new kind of species — "metal ion plus surrounding molecules". Structures of this kind are called complexes (also coordination compounds), and they have three essential parts:

  • Central ion (or atom): the metal presiding at the center, e.g. Cu²⁺, Fe³⁺ — the "tenant" that furnishes empty orbitals;
  • Ligand: the routers positioned around the center, e.g. H2O, NH3, CN⁻ — the atom directly bonded to the center is called the donor atom (N in NH3, C in CN⁻);
  • Coordination number: the number of donor atoms bonded directly to the center — commonly 4 (square planar / tetrahedral) and 6 (octahedral).

The bond between ligand and center is called a coordinate bond: the shared electron pair is provided entirely by the ligand, while the central ion contributes only an empty orbital. It is still a covalent bond at heart — just one with a single "investor". Note the direction of that sentence — the ligand supplies the electron pair, making it an expert donor; the central ion accepts the pair. This foreshadowing unfolds into the Lewis acid–base theory in Chapter 4.

Inner Sphere, Outer Sphere, and a First Look at Naming

When writing a complex's formula, use square brackets to enclose the "inner sphere" (the core held together by coordinate bonds); outside the brackets is the "outer sphere" (loosely associated through ionic bonds). For example, [Cu(NH3)4]SO4: inner sphere [Cu(NH3)4]2+ plus outer sphere SO42−. On dissolving in water the outer sphere dissociates, while the inner sphere essentially stays intact — so the solution shows the complex's own color.

For naming, memorize one template: (ligand-count prefix + ligand name + central metal name + oxidation state in Roman numerals in parentheses); if the complex ion is an anion, the metal name additionally takes the ending "-ate". Let's walk through two examples:

  • [Cu(NH3)4]SO4 → tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate (the outer-sphere sulfate is named last; tetraammine = 4 NH3; copper is in oxidation state II);
  • K3[Fe(CN)6] → potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III) (the inner sphere is an anion, so the metal name takes "-ate" — ferrate; iron is in oxidation state III).
Example 1 · Full Demonstration (Watch Me): Taking K₃[Fe(CN)₆] Apart
Identify the inner and outer spheres of K3[Fe(CN)6], the central ion and its oxidation state, the ligand and donor atom, and the coordination number — then name the compound.
  1. Find the brackets: inside the square brackets, [Fe(CN)6]3− is the inner sphere; the 3 K+ outside are the outer sphere. The outer-sphere charge is opposite and equal in magnitude → the inner sphere carries −3.
  2. Fix the central oxidation state: inside, the 6 CN− total −6 and the inner sphere is −3; let iron be x: x + (−6) = −3 → x = +3, so the center is Fe³⁺.
  3. Identify the ligand: the ligand is cyanide CN−; the donor atom is carbon, C (the lone pair sits at the C end), and each CN⁻ contributes 1 donor atom.
  4. Count the coordination number: the 6 CN⁻ each contribute one C → coordination number = 6, giving octahedral geometry.
  5. ✅ Name it: the inner sphere is an anion → the metal takes "-ate": potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III). Self-check: the 3 outer-sphere K+ (+3) exactly neutralize the inner sphere's −3, so the compound is electrically neutral ✓

Think It Through: CO carries no charge and shows no basicity — on what grounds can it serve as a ligand? The chemistry of carbon-monoxide poisoning hides in this very question

Hint: a ligand's ticket of admission is not charge but a lone pair. Does CO's carbon end have one?

Yes — and an exceptionally strong one. The lone pair on CO's carbon end not only coordinates but can also accept d electrons back from the metal (a "back bond"), giving it more than 200 times the binding strength of O2 for the Fe²⁺ in heme — and that is exactly how carbon-monoxide poisoning works: CO commandeers the oxygen-transport seat, and O2 is left queuing outside. The key to emergency treatment is a hyperbaric oxygen chamber, whose high partial pressure pushes CO off the site. Victory in ligand competition often comes down to how strongly each lone pair "plays its hand" — remember this picture; you will meet it again in the acid–base contest of Chapter 4.

§3 A First Look at Crystal Field Theory: d-Orbital Splitting Makes Color

The source of all this color is a question of geometry. In a free metal ion the 5 d orbitals (dxy, dxz, dyz, dz², dx²−y²) are exactly equal in energy; but when 6 ligands close in from six directions (octahedral geometry), the two orbitals that point at the ligands (dz² and dx²−y², together called eg) run head-on into the negative charge clouds and are pushed up in energy, while the three that keep clear of the ligands (dxy and its companions, together called t2g) drop relatively lower — this is d-orbital splitting, and the energy gap between the two sets is written Δo (the splitting energy).

🪜 Three-Step Ladder · A colorless ion plus ligands — where does the color come from?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Think of white light as a full row of piano keys. An ordinary free ion is like an unretuned piano — there is no pitch difference between the keys, so no visible light can "play" it, and it stays colorless. When ligands arrive, it is as if someone raised two of the keys: a fixed pitch difference now exists. Light whose energy exactly matches that difference gets absorbed (the note sounds); a band of color goes missing from the white light, and your eye sees the remaining complementary color. The pitch difference (Δ) is set by the ligand — swapping ligands means retuning the piano.

LEVEL 2 · d–d transition

Back to the electrons: the ligand field splits the 5 d orbitals into an upper set (eg) and a lower set (t2g) separated by Δ. An electron of Cu²⁺ (d⁹) can absorb a photon whose energy exactly equals Δ and jump from the lower set to the upper — this is the d–d transition. As white light passes through, the absorbed band vanishes, and the solution shows the complementary color of what was taken out. For the aqua copper ion, Δ corresponds to absorbing orange-red light → you see blue; switch to NH3 ligands and Δ grows, shifting absorption to shorter wavelengths. The solution commonly appears deep blue, but Δ alone does not determine color intensity.

LEVEL 3 · Quantitative and Exceptions

Photon energy E = hν = hc/λ: the larger Δ, the shorter the absorbed wavelength, and the observed color shifts by the complement rule. In the ligand-strength ranking (a spectrochemical-series fragment) H2O < NH3 < CN−, CN− is strongest because, beyond electrostatics, its π system can siphon off metal d electrons (back-donation), widening the split further. Two important exceptions to remember: ① when the d orbitals are completely full or completely empty (e.g. Zn²⁺ d¹⁰, Sc³⁺ d⁰) there are no electrons to jump, or no vacancies for d–d transitions; these complexes are usually colorless if there is no other visible absorption; ② yet MnO4− (Mn +7, d⁰) is intensely purple — that is not a d–d transition but the stronger "charge transfer" (an O electron momentarily jumps toward Mn), whose absorption is a hundred times more intense and whose color is far more vivid.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · "A substance's color comes from added pigments"
✗
Misconception A blue bottle holds a blue dye, a green solution has green pigment stirred in — copper(II) sulfate solution is so blue, surely some "copper pigment" has dissolved into it.
✓
Correct The color of a transition-metal solution needs no pigment at all: d electrons absorb specific visible bands according to Δ (the d–d transition), and the remaining light enters your eye — color is the negative of selective absorption (the complementary color). Cu²⁺ absorbs orange-red and looks blue; Fe³⁺ absorbs violet-blue and looks yellow; MnO4− absorbs yellow-green and looks purple. The proof is that the color changes with Δ once ligands are added — if it were merely "mixed-in pigment", swapping ligands should not swap colors.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · "The color deepened when ammonia was added — it's just more concentrated, right?"
✗
Misconception Pale blue to deep blue — same as brewing stronger coffee. The ammonia just "concentrated" the Cu²⁺; the copper ion is still the same copper ion.
✓
Correct What changed is the chemical species itself: the ligand went from H2O to NH3, [Cu(H2O)4]2+ → [Cu(NH3)4]2+. The NH3 ligand field is stronger → Δ is larger → the absorption peak shifts to shorter wavelengths. The full absorption spectrum, concentration, and path length determine the observed color and its intensity. Think It Through: Zn²⁺ also forms the complex [Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺ with NH₃ — so why is its solution nearly colorless?

Hint: count the d electrons on a Zn atom (Zn is element 30: Ar core + 3d¹⁰4s²).

Zn²⁺ is d¹⁰, completely full: the lower set of orbitals is fully occupied — and so is the upper set — leaving no vacancy for an electron to jump into, so the d–d transition cannot happen; visible light is barely absorbed and the solution is colorless. Likewise Sc³⁺ (d⁰) has no electrons to jump and is also colorless. Turned around, the color itself leaks information about the d-electron count: only with d between 0 and 10 is there a "stage" to jump onto. That is why "colored" is the d-block's signature, while the s block and the zinc group stand out by going bare-faced.

🧯 Cyanide Safety Warning (Must Read)
The CN⁻ in this section is strictly on paper — never attempt any experiment involving cyanides yourself. CN⁻ coordinates extremely strongly: inside the body it locks tightly onto the Fe³⁺ in cytochrome oxidase, crashing the respiratory chain on the spot; a few milligrams can be fatal, and contact with acid releases the highly toxic gas HCN. In industry, cyanide-containing wastewater must be detoxified by oxidation before discharge. "The strongest coordinating ability" is a beautiful blue in a test tube — and a deadly robbery inside the body; the power of chemistry cuts both ways.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • The three hallmarks of the transition metals share one root: flexible d-electron energies → multiple oxidation states (Mn reaches +7), color (d–d transitions), and catalysis (ammonia synthesis, exhaust cleanup, enzyme active centers).
  • The three essentials of a complex: central ion (furnishes empty orbitals), ligands (furnish lone pairs), coordination number (commonly 4 square planar, 6 octahedral); the coordinate bond is a covalent bond with a "single investor".
  • Inside the square brackets is the inner sphere (held together by coordinate bonds); outside is the outer sphere. Naming template: "ligand-count prefix + ligand name + metal name (oxidation state)", with an "-ate" ending when the inner sphere is an anion.
  • A first look at crystal field theory: the ligands split the 5 d orbitals into two sets with energy gap Δ; electrons absorb visible light of energy exactly Δ (a d–d transition), and the solution appears in the complementary color of the absorbed light.
  • Ligand-strength fragment H2O < NH3 < CN−: change the ligand → change Δ → change the color; d⁰/d¹⁰ are colorless; the intense purple of MnO4− comes from stronger charge transfer.
  • CO poisoning and cyanide poisoning are both ligand competition for Fe sites — coordination chemistry is quite literally a matter of life and death.
