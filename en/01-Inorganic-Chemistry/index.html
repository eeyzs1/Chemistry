Inorganic Chemistry: A Complete Map of the World of Elements

The water running from your tap, the salt in the pot, the stainless-steel watch case on your wrist, the iodine in a hospital CT contrast agent, the lithium in a battery—turn everyday life upside down and you will find that nearly everything beyond the carbon skeleton is inorganic chemistry's territory. In 4 chapters, this branch sets out from the periodic table's "terrain rules," visits the main-group elements on its two wings and the transition metals at its center, and finally dives into the most common and most important arena for reactions—the aqueous solution.

4 chapters · about 3.5 hours total Prerequisites: 00 Foundations of Chemistry Interactive animations ×6 20 end-of-chapter self-test questions in all

🧭 What you will learn in this branch

Inorganic chemistry studies every element and compound other than compounds built on carbon–hydrogen skeletons—from the lightest hydrogen to artificially synthesized superheavy elements, from simple table salt to structurally exquisite coordination compounds. Its boundary with organic chemistry (Branch 02) is not absolute: simple carbon compounds such as carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide are conventionally assigned to inorganic chemistry.

After completing this branch, you will be able to:

  • Read the periodic table's four major trends (atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity) and predict an element's temperament from its position;
  • Explain why s-block metals react so violently (why sodium dances on water) and why p-block nonmetals are so changeable (the bonding repertoire of the halogens and of nitrogen, phosphorus, oxygen, and sulfur);
  • Explain why transition metals are colorful and rich in oxidation states, and use entry-level concepts of coordination chemistry to read the deep blue of [Cu(NH3)4]2+;
  • Master pH, weak electrolyte equilibria, buffer solutions, and precipitation–dissolution—the shared foundation beneath analytical chemistry, environmental chemistry, and biochemistry.

🗺️ Prerequisites and study path

Prerequisites: 00 Foundations of Chemistry (atomic structure, chemical bonding, the mole and stoichiometry, reaction energy). At a minimum you should be able to answer: "How many electrons does Mg2+ have?" "How many grams are in 1 mol of water?"—if these are not yet automatic, it is wise to return to Branch 00 for a refresher before setting out.

The four chapters follow a carefully designed route—please do not skip chapters:

  1. Chapter 1 · The Periodic Law—first pick up "map-reading skills": every later phenomenon can be traced back to periodic trends;
  2. Chapter 2 · Main-Group Elements—take the trend toolkit into the field across the s and p blocks, explaining the liveliest reactions of the elements;
  3. Chapter 3 · Transition Metals and Coordination Chemistry—push into the d block at the table's center for a first look at the world of color and complexes;
  4. Chapter 4 · Acids, Bases, and Aqueous Chemistry—usher all the earlier "solids and gases" into water, and learn to describe equilibria in solution quantitatively.
📌 Route tips
Each chapter takes about 45–55 minutes (animations and self-test included). If you score below 60 on a chapter's self-test, reread the summary and the misconception smasher before pressing on— concepts in this branch interlock, and Chapter 4 leans especially hard on the electronegativity intuition built in Chapter 1.

🌏 Sub-discipline map: going deeper into inorganic chemistry

This branch is your entry ticket. As a top-level discipline, inorganic chemistry contains these further directions—after finishing this branch you will be qualified to go deeper as your interests lead:

  • Coordination chemistry: how metal centers and ligands "shake hands" into molecules—the deep water directly beyond Chapter 3;
  • Solid-state inorganic chemistry: the structure and properties of the "hard stuff"—crystals, ceramics, superconductors, and battery electrode materials;
  • Inorganic synthesis: the art of "how to make it"—preparation under extreme conditions such as high temperature, high pressure, and rigorously water- and oxygen-free environments;
  • Descriptive chemistry: a systematic, family-by-family and element-by-element inventory of "what reacts with what, and what forms"—the chemist's dictionary;
  • Bioinorganic chemistry: the metals of life—iron in heme, magnesium in chlorophyll, cobalt in vitamin B12;
  • Inorganic materials chemistry: the crossroads serving semiconductors, catalysts, and nanomaterials, linking the chemical industry with manufacturing.
