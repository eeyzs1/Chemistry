01 Inorganic Chemistry · Chapter 4

Acids, Bases, and Aqueous Solutions: The Arena Where Every Reaction Plays Out

On a heartburn night you swallow an antacid tablet, and minutes later the "fire" in your stomach is out — what that little white tablet performs is one of the oldest reactions in chemistry: neutralization. But what exactly is an "acid"? What exactly is a "base"? Humanity has answered this question three times, each answer deeper than the last. This chapter climbs a three-step ladder to meet Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry, and Lewis, learns to fit the whole aqueous world with a pH scale, then watches weak electrolytes yield only halfway, buffers stand rock-steady, and precipitates walk a tightrope between dissolving and dropping out.

Introduction: The Chemistry of an Antacid Tablet

Your stomach secretes about 1.5 liters of gastric juice every day, its hydrochloric acid concentration about 0.1 mol/L and its pH as low as 1.5–2 — enough to activate digestive enzymes and kill the overwhelming majority of bacteria that invade with food. The stomach wall protects itself with a mucus–bicarbonate barrier, but once the barrier fails (late nights, spicy food, drug irritation), the hydrochloric acid burns into the stomach lining — that is "heartburn".

The antacid's method is plain and effective: neutralize the excess acid with the basic aluminum hydroxide and magnesium carbonate (Al(OH)3 + 3HCl → AlCl3 + 3H2O). But chase one follow-up question and the whole chapter tumbles out: aluminum hydroxide acts as a base toward acid, yet it ionizes to release no OH⁻ and contains no water — on what grounds does it count as a "base"? Humanity has given three successive answers to the definition of "acid and base", each covering wider ground. And to measure how far neutralization has gone, we still need a ruler: pH.

§1 Three-Stage Evolution of Acid–Base Theory: From "Origin" to "Electrons"

The three theories do not refute one another — they are three concentric circles: the innermost is the most intuitive, the outermost the most general, and each outward expansion absorbs the corners the previous circle could not explain. Climb the ladder one rung at a time:

🪜 Three-step ladder · What exactly are acids and bases?
1884 · ionization in water

Arrhenius: an acid = a substance that ionizes in water to release H+; a base = a substance that ionizes in water to release OH−. Hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, and caustic soda (NaOH) all fall neatly into place, and neutralization is naturally H+ + OH− → H2O. Intuitive and convenient, this is the whole of middle-school chemistry; but it soon runs into stubborn holdouts — ammonia solution is plainly a base, yet the NH3 molecule contains no OH⁻; its OH⁻ is seized from water, not ionized out of itself.

1923 · proton transfer

Brønsted–Lowry: an acid = a donor of protons (H+); a base = a proton acceptor. NH3 accepts H+ to become NH4+, qualifying beyond dispute as a base. The essence of acid–base reactions is unified into one sentence: the proton moves house from the acid to the base. What remains after an acid releases its proton is called its conjugate base; a base that gains a proton becomes its conjugate acid — for example HAc / Ac− and NH4+ / NH3, a "conjugate acid–base pair" differing by exactly one proton. The stronger the conjugate acid, the weaker its conjugate base (a strong acid is paired with a weak base).

1923 · electron pairs

Lewis: an acid = an electron-pair acceptor; a base = an electron-pair donor. An acid–base reaction = one side supplies an empty orbital, the other a lone pair, and a bond forms. The coordinate bonds of Chapter 3 are textbook Lewis acid–base reactions: Cu²⁺ (Lewis acid, empty orbitals) meets NH3 (Lewis base, the lone pair on N), and the deep-blue [Cu(NH3)4]2+ appears. Even proton-free BF3 + NH3 is brought into the fold. The three layers nest: Arrhenius ⊂ Brønsted ⊂ Lewis — the outer circle explains everything the inner one can, plus everything the inner one cannot. Modern inorganic chemistry takes one more step with "hard and soft acids and bases" theory, refining the art of donor–acceptor matching — a story for another day.

Think It Through: sodium bicarbonate, an ingredient of stomach medicines, also counts as a "base" — yet HCO₃⁻ can even act as an "acid". What exactly is it?

Hint: write its reactions with H⁺ and with OH⁻ respectively, and see which way the proton moves.

Toward an acid (gastric acid) it is a base: HCO3− + H+ → H2CO3 → CO2↑ + H2O (the burp after taking the medicine is CO₂ escaping); toward a base it is an acid: HCO3− + OH− → CO32− + H2O. A substance that can work at both ends is called an amphoteric substance, rooted in the conjugate chain H2CO3 / HCO3− / CO32− — HCO3− is both the conjugate base of carbonic acid and the conjugate acid of the carbonate ion. Blood pH is steadied by exactly this buffering pair (§2).

§2 pH: Fitting Aqueous Solutions with a Ruler

It all begins with water's own little secret: pure water faintly swaps protons with itself — H2O ⇌ H+ + OH−, an autoionization. At 25 °C only about one molecule in five hundred million does so, but that is already enough to establish an iron law, the ion product of water:

Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1×10−14 (25 °C)

In other words, in any aqueous solution H+ and OH− always coexist, and their product is constant: acidity is merely H+ holding the upper hand (taking the lion's share of the 10−14 budget), basicity is OH− on top. pHpotential of hydrogen turns this "division of the spoils" into a handy ruler:

pH = −lg[H+] (mol/L)

Every drop of 1 in pH means [H+] rises tenfold — the H+ concentration of gastric acid (pH ≈ 1.5) is more than three hundred thousand times that of pure water (pH 7). From this come the criteria for neutral, acidic, and basic: [H+] = [OH−] is neutral; [H+] > [OH−] is acidic; the reverse is basic.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · pH = 7 always means neutral
✗
Misconception Neutral means pH 7 — common knowledge written into the textbooks; does it really need doubting? Setting a solution's pH to 7 makes it neutral.
✓
Correct "pH = 7 means neutral" is only a coincidence at 25 °C. The true criterion for neutrality is [H+] = [OH−]. The ionization of water is endothermic, so Kw grows as temperature rises: at 100 °C, Kw ≈ 5×10−13, pure water has [H+] = [OH−] ≈ 7×10−7, and pH ≈ 6.14 — yet it is still perfectly, unambiguously neutral. The number 7 is a function of temperature; the equality is the essence.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · there is no OH⁻ in an acidic solution
✗
Misconception Since the solution is acidic, OH⁻ should have been "wiped out" by H⁺ — so in an acidic solution [OH⁻] = 0.
✓
Correct Kw is a product that never clocks out: as long as the solution is aqueous, [H+][OH−] stays fixed at 10−14 (25 °C). In an acid at pH 2, [OH−] = 10−14/10−2 = 10−12 mol/L — it is there, merely squeezed to an extreme low. Conversely, in a basic solution at pH 12, [H+] = 10−12, and it too never reaches zero. What never gets wiped out is not the opposing ion but the rule itself: "the product must hold constant".

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: calculating pH

Example 1 · Full demonstration (watch me do it): the pH of 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid
Calculate the pH of a 0.01 mol/L HCl solution, and find [OH−] in that solution (25 °C).
  1. Classify strong vs. weak: HCl is a strong acid and ionizes completely in water: HCl → H+ + Cl−; every HCl molecule hands over one H⁺.
  2. Find [H⁺]: [H⁺] = 0.01 mol/L = 1×10−2 mol/L. (The 10−7 contributed by water's own ionization is negligible by comparison.)
  3. Substitute into the definition: pH = −lg[H⁺] = −lg(10−2) = 2.
  4. Use Kw to find [OH⁻]: [OH⁻] = Kw/[H⁺] = 10−14/10−2 = 1×10−12 mol/L — OH⁻ is still present in the acidic solution, just pressed extremely low.
  5. ✅ Check and extend: pH + pOH = 2 + 12 = 14 ✓. Diluting this solution 100-fold raises the pH to 4; but infinite dilution never crosses 7 — water's own 10⁻⁷ eventually takes over (it must be counted in very dilute solutions).

Weak electrolytes: acetic acid's halfhearted ionization

Acetic acid (CH3COOH, abbreviated HAc) is not so forthright: it is a weak acid and only partly ionizes in water — HAc ⇌ H+ + Ac− — and at equilibrium the bulk of the solution is still intact HAc molecules. That is why 0.1 mol/L acetic acid has a pH of only about 2.9 (while hydrochloric acid of the same concentration gives 1): the molecules "dissociate and recombine" as they go, reaching a dynamic equilibrium. This "⇌" is why the titration curve in §3 starts out flat.

The secret of buffer solutions hides in this very equilibrium: stock a large reserve of a conjugate acid–base pair at the same time (for example HAc and its sodium salt NaAc), and small amounts of incoming strong acid or base are digested in turn by the reserve army while the pH barely moves — blood relies on H2CO3/HCO3− to hold its pH at 7.35–7.45, where a deviation of 0.4 units becomes life-threatening. Preparing a buffer uses the common-ion effect: add a strong electrolyte containing a shared ion to the weak electrolyte solution (NaAc brings Ac−), the ionization equilibrium shifts left, and the degree of ionization of HAc decreases. For the quantitative calculations see the 03 Analytical Chemistry branch; here it is enough to think the principle through.

Example 2 · Guided practice (do it with me): why a buffer stands rock-steady
Add 0.01 mol of HCl to 1 L of pure water and the pH crashes from 7 to 2; add the same amount of acid to 1 L of buffer containing HAc and NaAc (both 0.1 mol/L) and the pH hardly changes. Why?
  1. 💡 Hint: what is the buffer's inventory? — Large coexisting stocks of a conjugate acid–base pair: HAc (the acid store) and Ac− (the base store), connected by the equilibrium HAc ⇌ H+ + Ac−.
  2. 💡 Hint: an incoming strong acid = a sudden surge of H⁺. Which way does the equilibrium move? — Left: the stockpiled Ac− immediately combines with H+ to form HAc, "swallowing" the foreign acid into the acid store. Pure water has no base store, so its pH is left free to crash.
  3. 💡 Hint: an incoming strong base = a sudden surge of OH⁻. The OH⁻ first consumes H⁺, and the equilibrium promptly shifts right: the acid store HAc ionizes some more, giving the H⁺ back and cancelling the base's impact.
  4. 💡 Hint: why stress "a small amount"? Each storekeeper holds only 0.1 mol of stock, and the outside help (0.01 mol) can be absorbed calmly only while it stays within the inventory; pour in 1 mol of strong acid and the base store is emptied — the buffer fails at once.
  5. ✅ Check the logic: a buffer = a generous reserve of a conjugate pair + the spontaneous shift of an equilibrium; the conjugate base defends against acid, the conjugate acid against base. Blood's H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ pair keeps watch day and night in exactly this way (for quantitative calculations see the 03 Analytical Chemistry branch).
📌 Three mantras of this chapter
① The essence of an acid–base reaction: a proton moving house (Brønsted) or an electron pair forming a bond (Lewis). ② A pH difference of 1 = a concentration difference of 10; [H⁺] and [OH⁻] share a conserved product of 10⁻¹⁴, and neither ever goes to zero. ③ A buffer = a generous reserve of a conjugate acid–base pair: the base wards off incoming acid, the acid wards off incoming base.

§3 Titration Curves and Precipitation–Dissolution: Two Quantitative Windows

Once pH is in hand, we can draw a full portrait of "how far neutralization has gone": the acid–base titration. Titrate 20.00 mL of 0.1000 mol/L HCl with 0.1000 mol/L NaOH, record the pH drop by drop, and you get the most famous S-shaped curve in chemistry. Think It Through: why does the curve "take off vertically" near the equivalence point while lying almost flat before and after?

Hint: work out [H⁺] at 19.95 mL and at 20.05 mL — half a drop apart, so by what factor does the concentration differ?

At 19.95 mL the acid is slightly in excess and [H⁺] ≈ 2.5×10⁻⁴ mol/L; at 20.05 mL the base is slightly in excess, [OH⁻] ≈ 2.5×10⁻⁴, which converts to [H⁺] ≈ 4×10⁻¹¹. Half a drop apart, [H⁺] differs by nearly a factor of ten million — of course the pH jumps upright. Before that, acid is in large excess and a few drops of base merely shave a little off "a lot", so the relative change is small and the pH creeps up; the equivalence point is the handover threshold of "who is in excess", where the dominant ion steps straight from H⁺ to OH⁻ with no transition zone in between. An indicator's job is to change color on exactly this steep slope — the "flash of pink" your eye catches rests on a transition across seven orders of magnitude.

Precipitation–dissolution equilibrium: a solid's "saturation humidity"

Acid–base is not the only equilibrium in aqueous solution. Drip AgNO3 solution into NaCl solution and white AgCl precipitate forms at once — but the reaction has not "ended cleanly": dissolution and deposition proceed simultaneously on the precipitate's surface, until a precipitation–dissolution equilibrium is reached:

AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) Ksp = [Ag+][Cl−]

The solubility productKsp changes only with temperature, and the smaller its value the harder the salt is to dissolve (AgCl about 1.8×10−10). To judge whether a precipitate will form, compare two numbers: the ion product Q of the current solution and Ksp — Q > Ksp and a precipitate deposits; Q = Ksp and the solution is exactly saturated; Q < Ksp and nothing settles out. With this rule, chemists precipitate impurities away for purification, put silver halides to work in photographic imaging, and let the F− in toothpaste replace the more soluble hydroxyapatite in tooth enamel with the less soluble fluorapatite to fight cavities. The formation and dissolution of precipitates is one more branch of the grand theme of "equilibrium" at the solid–liquid interface — sharing the same way of thinking as ionization equilibria and titration curves.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • The three definitions of acids and bases expand ring by ring: Arrhenius (ionize in water to H⁺/OH⁻) ⊂ Brønsted–Lowry (proton donor/acceptor, conjugate acid–base pairs) ⊂ Lewis (electron-pair acceptor/donor, covering coordination reactions).
  • The ion product of water Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10−14 (25 °C): the two ions always coexist and their product is conserved; the criterion for neutrality is that they are equal, not that pH is exactly 7.
  • pH = −lg[H⁺]: strong acids and bases ionize completely, so substitute directly; a pH difference of 1 = a concentration difference of 10.
  • A weak acid (HAc) ionizes partially into a dynamic equilibrium; a buffer solution = a generous reserve of a conjugate acid–base pair, resisting both acid and base; the common-ion effect decreases its degree of ionization.
  • A titration curve jumps several pH units within half a drop near the equivalence point, so an indicator must change color inside the jump region (for the quantitative treatment see the 03 Analytical Chemistry branch).
  • Ksp qualitatively: compare Q with Ksp to decide whether a solid forms or dissolves — precipitation, purification, and cavity prevention follow the same principle.
