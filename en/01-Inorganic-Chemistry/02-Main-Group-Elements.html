01 Inorganic Chemistry · Chapter 2

Main-Group Elements: The View from the Two Wings of the Periodic Table

The sparklers on a birthday cake, the helium–oxygen breathing gas of deep-sea divers, the tiny flame in a lighter flint, the carmine strontium in fireworks — they all come from the two wings of the periodic table. Armed with the "map of trends" from Chapter 1, this chapter surveys the s block and the p block in the field: the left wing is the family of alkali metals that explode on contact with water; the right wing is a gradient belt running from the reactive halogens to the "can't-be-bothered" noble gases. You will find that within a group the personalities are strikingly alike, while between groups the temperaments differ wildly — and all of it can be derived from the tug-of-war between Zeff and the number of electron shells.

Introduction: A Family Reunion

Imagine two family reunions. At the s-block party on the left wing: lithium is fairly well-mannered, sodium picks a fight with the water tank the moment it walks in, and potassium sets the curtains on fire within a second of arriving — each one more hot-tempered than the last, with the temper rising down the group. The p-block party on the right wing is far livelier: the halogen family is snatching electrons, the carbon group is raising frameworks, nitrogen, phosphorus, oxygen, and sulfur each perform their bonding stunts, and in the corner the noble gases stand with folded arms, ignoring everyone.

Chapter 1 established that down a group, electron shells increase, radii grow, and IE falls — the metals become ever more "generous"; across a row from left to right, Zeff increases — the elements become ever more "stingy." The entire plot of this chapter is these two rules performing on real elements. We start with the most hot-tempered block of all: the s block.

§1 The s Block: The Fire and Fury of the Alkali and Alkaline-Earth Metals

The s block = columns 1–2 on the left wing: group ⅠA alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) and group ⅡA alkaline-earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba). Their shared portrait can be drawn straight from the Chapter 1 trends: the largest radii and the lowest IEs in the whole table — the 1–2 outermost electrons live "far from the throne," beyond the nucleus's easy reach, and are extremely easy to take away. As a result, all s-block metals are strong reducing agents: hydrogen with water, flames with oxygen, salts with halogens — there is no mild option.

Sodium and Water: The Classic of Classics

Drop a pea-sized piece of sodium into water tinged with phenolphthalein, and five phenomena follow in one seamless sequence — float, melt, dart, hiss, red. Memorize the mnemonic first; the animation below takes it apart frame by frame:

🪜 Three-Level Ladder · "Float, melt, dart, hiss, red" — not one step wasted
LEVEL 1 · Match Them Up

Read the five phenomena like a medical report, item by item: float = lighter than water; melt = melts exceptionally easily (low melting point) + the reaction releases heat; dart = a jet of gas at the tail (a gas is given off); hiss = hot and bubbling (heat + bursting bubbles); red = a basic "calling card" left behind (NaOH turns phenolphthalein red). Each phenomenon is a live broadcast of one property.

LEVEL 2 · The Data

Sodium's density is 0.97 g/cm³ (< water's 1.0) → it floats; its melting point is only 97.8 °C, and the heat released easily pushes the interface temperature past that → it melts into a ball; the reaction 2Na + 2H2O → 2NaOH + H2↑ keeps releasing hydrogen bubbles at the ball's surface, and when enough accumulate on one side they push the ball toward the other → it darts around; the heat makes the water boil locally and the bubbles burst → hissing; the NaOH formed dissolves completely → phenolphthalein turns red. Potassium is fiercer: it releases heat faster, often igniting the hydrogen outright with an explosive "firework" bang.

LEVEL 3 · The Essence

The reaction in electron terms: Na's IE is only 496 kJ/mol, so electrons pass directly from the metal surface to the H+ that water faintly ionizes to give, and H2 deposits on the ball's surface — in effect a short-circuited micro-battery. Hydrogen evolves at randomly distributed spots, the thrust keeps changing direction, and the ball scurries wildly; the heat concentrates at the millimeter-scale solid–liquid interface, so the local temperature sails past the melting point. Note that "water supplies H+" means pure water itself contains a few ions — exactly the starting point of the pH theory in Chapter 4.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · "All metals are heavier than water"
✗
Misconception Iron blocks, copper blocks, gold bars — toss any of them into water and they sink — "metal means heavy" seems only natural, so sodium, being a metal, must surely sink too.
✓
Correct Floating or sinking is decided by density, not by "being a metal." Density = packed mass ÷ occupied volume: alkali-metal atoms have enormous radii and weak metallic bonding (each atom contributes only 1 electron of "glue"), so their crystals pack remarkably "fluffy." Sodium's density is only 0.97 g/cm³, lighter than water, so it floats on the surface; lithium 0.53, potassium 0.86, rubidium 1.53 — the first three all float on water. Iron at 7.9 and gold at 19.3, naturally, sink straight to the bottom. Down a group the atoms get heavier but also fluffier: the two trends race, and volume usually wins.

Why Sodium Sleeps Under Kerosene

In the laboratory, sodium is always kept submerged in kerosene in brown bottles, fetched out with tweezers and cut with a knife — because sodium's enemies are everywhere: at room temperature it oxidizes the instant it meets O2 (forming Na2O; when heated and burning it becomes Na2O2), and it reacts violently with water. Kerosene is an inert mixture of hydrocarbons, contains no active hydrogen, and does not react with sodium; meanwhile sodium's density of 0.97 > kerosene's ~0.8, so it settles exactly at the bottom of the bottle, completely cut off from air and moisture. Incidentally: lithium (0.53) is lighter than kerosene and would float on it — an imperfect seal — so it is instead sealed in paraffin.

🧯 Safety Notes (read this carefully)
Sodium and potassium experiments must use small amounts (pea-sized), good ventilation, and safety goggles; handle with tweezers, never bare hands. If sodium catches fire, never use water or foam extinguishers (they would detonate the hydrogen), and do not use CO2 extinguishers either (the combustion product Na2O2 reacts with CO2 to release oxygen) — the correct response is to smother it with dry sand to cut off the air.

🔬 Try It Out: The Flame Test

Solutions of s-block (and Ca, Sr, Cu, etc.) metal ions are colorless in themselves, but when picked up on a platinum wire and burned in a burner flame, the flame takes on an element-specific color — the classic method for testing metal ions, and the secret behind firework palettes. Think It Through: Solutions of Na⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺ are colorless — so where does the flame's color come from?

Hint: Is the color "emitted" or "carried within"? Think about stage lights versus an ordinary light bulb.

The solutions are colorless because at room temperature the electrons all sit in the ground state and absorb no visible light. The flame excites electrons to higher levels; when they jump back down they each emit a photon whose frequency is set by the gap between the two levels (ΔE = hν). Sodium's transition lands right at 589 nm (yellow light), potassium's mainly at 766 nm (the violet edge of the visible, near ultraviolet) ... so the color is not a pigment the ion "carries," but light "emitted on the spot" in the flame. This is also why the flame test detects the "element" rather than its bonding form — whether the sodium comes from NaCl or Na₂CO₃, the spectral lines are identical.

§2 The p Block: The Gradient Belt from Boron to Krypton

The p block on the right wing spans columns 13–18 and is the most compositionally mixed strip of the entire table: at the upper left, boron is a metalloid; below it, aluminum is a metal; to the right, oxygen and chlorine are nonmetals; and at the far end the noble gases simply ignore everyone. The diagonal rule from Chapter 1 is on full display here: nonmetallic character is strongest at the upper right, metallic character at the lower left, with fence-sitting metalloids in between. This section fields three representative teams: the electron-grabbing halogens; the bond-shapeshifting nitrogen, phosphorus, oxygen, and sulfur; and the framework-raising carbon and silicon.

Halogens: Four Brothers in the Electron Grab

Group ⅦA halogens (F, Cl, Br, I) hold 7 outermost electrons, just one short of a full house — so each of the free elements is an oxidizer, snatching electrons on sight. But the four brothers are far from equal in strength, and a single rule from Chapter 1 ranks them: down the group, nonmetallic character weakens → oxidizing power F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2.

The most direct application of the oxidizing order is the displacement reaction: the stronger halogen can push the weaker one out of its salt solution, never the reverse. Example 1 below demonstrates in full; Example 2 uses the same rule to explain "why the hydrides rank the other way."

Example 1 · Full demonstration (watch me): chlorine water added to KBr and KI solutions
Chlorine water is added dropwise to separate solutions of KBr and KI. What reaction occurs in each? What is observed? Can Br2 displace Cl2 from a KCl solution?
  1. Call up the rule: the oxidizing power of the free halogens is F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2 (nonmetallic character weakens down the group). The free element with the stronger oxidizing power can oxidize the anion of the weaker one.
  2. Judge the direction: Cl2 is a stronger oxidizer than Br2, so Cl2 can oxidize Br− to Br2, itself being reduced to Cl−.
  3. Write the equations: Cl2 + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br2 (ionic equation Cl2 + 2Br− → 2Cl− + Br2).
  4. Record the observations: the solution turns from colorless to orange-yellow (the color of Br2 dissolved in water); with KI, likewise Cl2 + 2KI → 2KCl + I2, the solution turns brown-yellow, and adding starch solution turns it blue (the characteristic reaction of I2 with starch).
  5. ✅ Answer the reverse question: Br2 is a weaker oxidizer than Cl2 and cannot displace Cl2 from KCl — displacement is a one-way street of "the strong evicting the weak."
Example 2 · Guided practice (do it with me): ranking the hydrides by stability
Rank HF, HCl, HBr, and HI by thermal stability from strongest to weakest. Why is this exactly the reverse of the "hydrohalic acid" acidity ranking (acidity strongest for HI)?
  1. 💡 Hint: thermal stability is about how hard it is to break the H—X bond. What determines bond energy? Recall Chapter 1 — down a group, what happens to the atomic radius?
  2. 💡 Hint: from F to I the radius grows stepwise → the H—X bond lengthens stepwise → the bond energy falls stepwise (H—F ~565, H—Cl 431, H—Br 366, H—I 299 kJ/mol).
  3. 💡 Hint: the stronger the bond, the harder the decomposition → stability HF > HCl > HBr > HI. Cross-check with experiment: HF survives a thousand degrees almost untouched, while HI decomposes visibly at three hundred.
  4. 💡 Hint: "acidity" measures how easily the H—X bond is torn open in water to release H+ — the weaker the bond, the easier the release, so the acidity ranking runs exactly opposite, with HI strongest.
  5. ✅ Self-check: stability reads bond energy (stronger = more stable), acidity reads ease of bond breaking (weaker = more acidic) — one set of bond-energy data, read in two directions. Why HF is the "anomalously weakest acid" is saved for the quantitative treatment in Chapter 4.

Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Oxygen, Sulfur: The Shape-Shifters of Bonding

Sandwiched between the carbon group and the halogens, N, P, O, and S are the p block's "shape-shifting masters" — the same element can wear completely different bonds in different molecules:

  • Nitrogen N: N2 locks itself shut with a triple bond (bond energy a towering 945 kJ/mol) — which is why it fills 78% of the air yet stays "steady as a rock"; but switch to NH3, where nitrogen carries a lone pair, and it can both bind H+ to act as a base (Chapter 4) and "donate electrons" to metal ions as a coordinate bond (Chapter 3: [Cu(NH3)4]2+).
  • Phosphorus P: white phosphorus is a small tetrahedral P4 molecule of 4 P atoms (deadly poison, ignites spontaneously); red phosphorus is a polymer of countless hooked-together P chains (a regular in safety matches). The same element, a whole new personality — purely from "how it connects."
  • Oxygen O: the O2 double bond provisions the living world; O3 (ozone) huddles three atoms together and absorbs ultraviolet light as the stratosphere's sunscreen — one extra atom, a whole new temper.
  • Sulfur S: sulfur atoms are champions of bonding to themselves, 8 S atoms linking head-to-tail into the crown-shaped S8 ring; elastic sulfur drawing into threads is the proof of rings pulled open into chains. Add oxygen to get SO2/SO3, hydrate that to H2SO4 — the mother of industry.
Think It Through: N₂ fills 78% of the atmosphere, and even thunderstorms pry it open only for an instant — why is nitrogen so "reluctant to react"?

Hint: Compare the bond energies of N₂ and O₂: N≡N ~945, O=O ~498 kJ/mol.

Nitrogen's "laziness" is not inactivity but the prohibitive price of the triple bond: to react, bonds must break first, and breaking N≡N demands an energy toll of nearly a thousand kJ/mol, which almost no one can pay at room temperature. That is why nitrogen fixation became chemistry's century-defining problem — thunderstorm discharges, root-nodule bacteria, and the iron catalyst of the Haber process are all brokers "paying nitrogen's down payment." Once the down payment is settled and N is converted into NH3 and other compounds, nitrogen turns instantly reactive, feeding explosives, fertilizers, and proteins. Between inertness and activity lies nothing but a bond energy.

Carbon and Silicon: Artists of the Skeleton

Group ⅣA carbon is the Michelangelo of the bonding world: all 4 valence electrons go into covalent bonds — sp³ to build diamond's three-dimensional network (nature's hardest material), sp² to lay down graphite's layered sheets (conducting within layers, gliding between them — pencil leads and electrodes), and rolled up, carbon nanotubes. All of organic chemistry (branch 02) is an encyclopedia of carbon skeletons. Silicon, one floor down in the same group, also loves raising frameworks, though in a different style — the Misconception Buster below is their classic comparison.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · "CO₂ is a gas, so group-mate SiO₂ should be similar"
✗
Misconception Carbon and silicon both sit in group ⅣA, so their chemistry should be "one style per family": CO₂ is a freestanding small molecule and dry ice flies off at the first heat, so sand — SiO₂ — is probably also a volatile molecular crystal of small molecules.
✓
Correct "Family resemblance" grants tendencies, not structures. The C atom is small and its C=O double bond is short and strong; one carbon with two oxygens is perfectly content — CO₂ is a freestanding three-atom molecule, condensed into dry ice by weak intermolecular forces (sublimes at −78 °C). The Si atom is a whole size larger, and its Si=O double bond is much weaker, so it opts instead for 4 Si—O single bonds: each Si grips 4 O, each O bridges 2 Si ... extending without end into a three-dimensional network (covalent network solid). Quartz melts above 1600 °C, nearly two thousand degrees away from dry ice — same group does not mean same destiny: atomic size rewrites how atoms connect, and how atoms connect rewrites everything.

§3 Noble Gases: Even Lazy Aristocrats Can Be Wooed

In the table's rightmost column sit the noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn), once called "inert gases": outer shells full (2 electrons for He, 8 for the rest), the highest IEs in the table, essentially zero appetite for gaining electrons — unwilling to give, too lazy to take — so they were long held to form no compounds whatsoever.

The turning point came in 1962. After preparing O2+[PtF6]−, Neil Bartlett noticed: xenon's first ionization energy (1170 kJ/mol) is nearly identical to O2's (~1175) — if PtF6 could snatch electrons from oxygen, why not from xenon? He mixed Xe with PtF6 and at once obtained the orange-yellow solid Xe+[PtF6]−. The first noble-gas compound was born, and the dogma of "inertness" collapsed on the spot. Thereafter XeF2, XeF4, XeF6 and more followed: XeF2 is a stable white crystalline solid, a gentle yet potent oxidizer still used in organic synthesis and etching. Note the pattern: the ones that can be "turned" are the heavier noble gases with larger radii and more loosely held outer electrons (Xe, Kr, Rn); He, Ne, and Ar remain rock-solid — the further down, the farther the outer electrons from the nucleus, and the easier for a strong oxidizer to pry them away.

While we are at it, note their everyday posts: He — balloons and deep-sea breathing gas (barely soluble in blood, extremely light); Ne — the red glow of neon signs; Ar — protective gas in light bulbs and argon-arc welding (0.93% of air, cheap and inert); Kr/Xe — high-performance flash lamps and anesthesia research. "Laziness" itself is a use: not reacting is the best protection.

📌 The Two Wings of This Chapter, Side by Side
s block, left wing: large radii, low IEs, strongly reducing — violent hydrogen release with water, sodium sleeping under kerosene, flame colors identifying ions. p block, right wing: a gradient running from metalloids to the most reactive nonmetal F; halogen oxidizing power F>Cl>Br>I, with hydride stability falling in step; carbon and silicon raising covalent skeletons; noble gases "lazy" for good reason — yet XeF2 proves even lazy aristocrats can be wooed. Every direction still answers to the two trends of Chapter 1.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • s-block metals = strong reducing agents: reacting with water as 2M + 2H2O → 2MOH + H2↑, ever fiercer down the group; sodium in water — "float, melt, dart, hiss, red."
  • Sodium is lighter than water — and lighter still than the tempers of its "opponents" — so it must be stored isolated from air and moisture (under kerosene; lithium sealed in paraffin).
  • The flame test is physical light emission by electron transitions releasing photons; it identifies the element and is independent of its chemical form.
  • Halogen oxidizing power F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2: the strong displace the weak; hydride stability runs the same way (HF most stable), while acid strength runs exactly opposite (Chapter 4).
  • N/P/O/S change character by changing how they connect; carbon and silicon build skeletons of covalent single bonds — CO₂ small molecules vs SiO₂ three-dimensional networks is the textbook case of structure determining properties.
  • The noble gases' inertness comes from their filled shells; the existence of XeF2 shows that "inertness" is only relative — the heavier noble gases can be won over by strong oxidizers.
