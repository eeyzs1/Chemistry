Electrochemistry: The Exchange Counter Between Chemical and Electrical Energy

When your phone drops from 100% to 20%, the anxiety sets in. Inside that little battery, Chapters 1 and 2 are performing a duet: electron transfer (redox) supplies the energy difference, while an ingenious device stops the electrons from being handed over "on the spot" and instead makes them flow in one direction along a wire — and that is an electric current. This chapter begins with an 1836 copper–zinc cell: why is the salt bridge indispensable? How do electrode potentials predict the direction of a reaction? How can electrolysis "defy fate" and drive the non-spontaneous? And finally we watch Li⁺ swing back and forth like a rocking chair inside a lithium-ion battery.

Introduction: Putting "Fire" in a Box

Humans have used fire for hundreds of thousands of years: wood oxidizes and gives off light and heat — but the energy scatters in every direction, impossible to grasp or carry. In 1800 Volta stacked copper and zinc discs in brine and for the first time made redox happen quietly on metal surfaces, with the wire gathering the electrons into a current. From then on, "combustion" could be packed into a box: a car engine burning gasoline goes chemical energy → heat → mechanical work, while in a hydrogen fuel-cell vehicle the very same hydrogen–oxygen reaction delivers an electric current directly, roughly doubling the efficiency and leaving nothing but water in the exhaust.

Electrochemistry studies the two-way exchange between chemical energy ⇌ electrical energy: a spontaneous reaction (ΔG < 0) can be built into a battery that generates electricity, while a non-spontaneous reaction (ΔG > 0) can be driven through by electrolysis. The route for this chapter: first review electron bookkeeping (§1), then dissect the galvanic cell (§2), learn to predict direction with the potential table (§3), put that to work in reverse (§4), and finally step into the lithium battery in your pocket (§5).

§1 Redox Reactions: The Bookkeeping of Electrons

The oxidation number is the "book value" of electrons assigned to an atom. In a reaction, a rising oxidation number = losing electrons = being oxidized (that species is the reducing agent, supplying electrons to others); a falling oxidation number = gaining electrons = being reduced (that species is the oxidizing agent, collecting electrons from others). Electrons never vanish into thin air: whenever one species loses them another gains them, and the two ledgers must balance.

Watch this chapter's star reaction: dip a zinc strip into blue copper(II) sulfate solution, and red-brown copper slowly plates onto the zinc while the blue color of the solution fades:

Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu 　　Zn: 0 → +2 (loses 2e⁻, oxidized)　Cu: +2 → 0 (gains 2e⁻, reduced)

If you simply mix them, the electrons are transferred "on the spot" at the zinc–copper contact surface, and the energy escapes as heat. The whole trick of electrochemistry is to let oxidation and reduction happen at two separate sites, forcing the electrons to take the long way around through a wire — so energy that would have dissipated becomes a usable electric current.

Think It Through: An iron nail and a copper wire are dipped into dilute hydrochloric acid at the same time; bubbles appear only on the nail. Which one is losing electrons? What is the copper doing?

Hint: The bubbles are H₂ — H⁺ gains electrons and is reduced; so who is supplying the electrons?

The iron loses electrons: Fe − 2e⁻ → Fe²⁺, and the released electrons are picked up by H⁺ at the surface, producing H₂ bubbles. The copper does not react with dilute hydrochloric acid — it is far more "reluctant" than iron to give up electrons. This difference in how easily electrons are lost is exactly what electrode potentials (§3) quantify, and it is why in §2 zinc is happy to serve as the negative electrode while copper only serves as the positive one.

§2 Galvanic Cells: Splitting a Redox Reaction Apart

In 1836 Daniell split the reaction above into two halves: a zinc strip dips into ZnSO₄ solution (left), a copper strip into CuSO₄ solution (right); a salt bridge spans the two solutions and a wire connects the two metals — and the lamp lights up. Such a device is called a galvanic (voltaic) cell, and each half of the setup is a half-cell. Electrode naming is the first hurdle of this chapter:

ElectrodeReaction occurringWhere the electrons goIn the Daniell cell
Negative electrode = anodeOxidation (electron loss)Electrons flow out, through the external circuit to the positive electrodeZn electrode: Zn − 2e⁻ → Zn²⁺
Positive electrode = cathodeReduction (electron gain)Accepts the electrons arriving through the external circuitCu electrode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
📌 Memory Anchor
An-ox, red-cat — the anode sings oxidation: oxidation always occurs at the anode, reduction always at the cathode. As for whether the anode is also the "positive electrode", galvanic and electrolytic cells are exactly opposite — §4 dismantles that trap in detail.

🪜 Three-Level Ladder · What problem does the salt bridge actually solve?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Imagine a crew of porters carrying goods into a warehouse: if they only carry in and never clear out, the doorway soon clogs up and the whole crew grinds to a halt. The left beaker only produces Zn²⁺ (piling up positive charge), the right beaker only consumes Cu²⁺ (leaving excess negative ions behind); the charge books go unbalanced on both sides and the reaction slams on the brakes in an instant. The salt bridge is the "clearance channel" that settles both charge accounts in real time, so the carrying can go on and on.

LEVEL 2 · Standard

Electrons travel through the wire (the external circuit), but there are no free electrons in solution — solutions conduct solely by means of ions (the internal circuit). If the two beakers are not connected, Zn²⁺ piles up in the negative-electrode compartment while SO₄²⁻ becomes relatively excessive in the positive-electrode compartment; within an instant the accumulated potential difference is enough to cancel the chemical driving force, and the current stalls on a millisecond timescale. With a salt bridge filled with KCl gel in place: K⁺ migrates into the cathode compartment to fill the positive-charge deficit, while Cl⁻ migrates into the anode compartment to neutralize the surplus Zn²⁺; both solutions stay electrically neutral and the current continues. The salt bridge also stops Cu²⁺ from wandering over to the zinc electrode to react directly, preventing a chemical short circuit.

LEVEL 3 · Essence

A complete cell circuit = an electronic conductor (the metal wires) + an ionic conductor (the solutions and the salt bridge) connected in series, with the two kinds of current "changing trains" at the electrode surfaces: the negative electrode hands electrons to the wire and leaves ions to the solution; the positive electrode does the reverse. The salt bridge uses KCl rather than just any salt because K⁺ and Cl⁻ migrate at nearly identical rates, which squeezes the liquid junction potential at the interface between the two solutions down to a minimum so it cannot steal from the cell's voltage. Precision measurements even use a "chain" of salt bridges to step across gradually — half the engineering of electrochemistry is a wrestling match with interfaces.

Think It Through: Replace the salt bridge with a thick copper wire, with its two ends dipped into the two solutions — will the cell still work?

Hint: What particles carry the "current" in solution? And in a copper wire?

It will not work properly. A copper wire is an electronic conductor, whereas electrons cannot migrate freely through a solution — the internal circuit needs an ion channel. The moment the copper wire enters the solutions it becomes a new electrode itself, triggering side reactions and extra interface potentials that scramble the cell voltage. The key to the salt bridge is not "conducting" as such, but "conducting by means of ions".

§3 Electrode Potentials: Predicting the Direction of a Reaction

Zinc is "willing" to lose electrons, copper is "unwilling" — and this difference can be quantified. Each redox couple (oxidized form/reduced form, e.g. Cu²⁺/Cu) is assigned a standard electrode potential (E°): measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) taken as 0 V, with ion concentrations of 1 mol/L at 25 °C. A few commonly used values:

CoupleE° / VTemperament
Zn²⁺ / Zn−0.76The reduced form, Zn, is generous — it loses electrons easily (an active metal)
Fe²⁺ / Fe−0.44Iron also gives up electrons, but is more reserved than zinc
Cu²⁺ / Cu+0.34The oxidized form, Cu²⁺, has an appetite for grabbing electrons
Cl₂ / Cl⁻+1.36Cl₂ is a strong oxidizing agent with a huge appetite for electrons

The ranking idea in one sentence: the larger E° is, the stronger the oxidized form (the more eager to gain electrons); the smaller E° is, the stronger the reduced form (the more eager to lose electrons). Combine two couples into a cell and judging the direction takes just three steps (see the example below); the criterion is E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode > 0 for a spontaneous reaction. It speaks the same language as the ΔG criterion of Chapter 1: ΔG° = −nFE° — when E° is positive, ΔG° must be negative. Electrochemistry is really thermodynamics speaking with a "voltage accent".

The Nernst Equation: Concentration "Discounts" the Voltage

The standard potential corresponds to the ideal concentration of 1 mol/L. When real concentrations deviate, the potential is corrected by the Nernst equation:

E = E° + (RT/nF)·ln([oxidized]/[reduced])

Qualitatively there are just two rules: oxidized-form concentration ↑ → E rises; reduced-form concentration ↑ → E falls. Use them to explain a battery's "aging": during discharge, Zn²⁺ keeps accumulating in the anode compartment, so Eanode rises; Cu²⁺ keeps being depleted in the cathode compartment, so Ecathode falls. Squeezed from both ends, Ecell = Ecathode − Eanode keeps shrinking and the voltage slides all the way down — when a long-used phone's charge reading "dives", that is the Nernst equation keeping the books.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Using the Potential Table to Predict Reaction Direction

Example 1 · Full Demonstration (Watch Me)
Given E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V, E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V, and E°(Fe²⁺/Fe) = −0.44 V. (1) Determine whether Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu is spontaneous; (2) determine whether Fe + Zn²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Zn is spontaneous.
  1. Find the two couples and compare: (1) involves Zn²⁺/Zn (−0.76) and Cu²⁺/Cu (+0.34). Rule: for the couple with the larger E°, its oxidized form serves as the oxidizing agent (gains electrons, acts as the positive electrode); for the couple with the smaller E°, its reduced form serves as the reducing agent (loses electrons, acts as the negative electrode). Here Cu²⁺ gains electrons and Zn loses them — exactly the direction written in the problem.
  2. Compute the cell emf: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V. E° > 0 → spontaneous — this is precisely the voltage of the Daniell cell in §2.
  3. Cross-check with thermodynamics: ΔG° = −nFE° = −2 × 96485 × 1.10 ≈ −212 kJ/mol < 0, fully consistent with Chapter 1's "ΔG < 0 means spontaneous" — the potential criterion and the free-energy criterion are two faces of the same coin.
  4. (2) Fe + Zn²⁺: taken as written, the positive electrode would be Zn²⁺/Zn (−0.76) and the negative electrode Fe²⁺/Fe (−0.44), so E°cell = −0.76 − (−0.44) = −0.32 V < 0 → not spontaneous; only the reverse reaction (Zn + Fe²⁺) is spontaneous. ✅ Check your intuition: zinc is more active than iron, so of course zinc is the one losing electrons — the direction criterion agrees with the activity series of metals.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · A battery is a box full of electrons?
✗
Misconception "A battery is like a reservoir, storing a full tank of electrons; charging pours the electrons back in, and when they are used up it is empty."
✓
Correct What a battery stores is chemical energy — high-energy active materials (zinc and copper ions; lithium and layered oxides). Electrons are merely the flowing carriers: during discharge they surge from the negative electrode through the external circuit to the positive electrode, circulating around the loop with their number never increasing or decreasing; what actually gets consumed is the material that is "willing to lose electrons". Charging is not pouring electrons back in either — it is an external supply pushing the reaction in reverse to regenerate the high-energy materials, banking the energy back in chemical form.

§4 Electrolysis and Faraday's Law: Defying Fate

A galvanic cell generates electricity from a spontaneous reaction; electrolysis turns this around: an external power supply forcibly drives a non-spontaneous reaction (ΔG > 0). In an electrolytic cell, the electrode connected to the positive terminal of the supply undergoes oxidation (it is the anode), and the electrode connected to the negative terminal undergoes reduction (it is the cathode) — note that this is the reverse of the galvanic cell:

Anode (oxidation)Cathode (reduction)
Galvanic cell (spontaneous, generates electricity)Negative electrode (e.g. Zn)Positive electrode (e.g. Cu)
Electrolytic cell (non-spontaneous, consumes electricity)Connected to the positive terminal of the supplyConnected to the negative terminal of the supply
⚠️ Misconception Buster · Anode = positive electrode?
✗
Misconception "The anode is the positive electrode and the cathode the negative — memorize it once and it works everywhere."
✓
Correct Anode and cathode are defined by reaction type (oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode), while positive and negative electrodes are defined by potential. In a galvanic cell the anode happens to be the negative electrode (Zn); in an electrolytic cell the anode is the one connected to the positive terminal. Ask "oxidation or reduction?" first, positive or negative second, and the two naming systems will never be mixed up.

Faraday's Law: The Exchange Rate Between Charge and Matter

In 1833 Faraday discovered that the amount of substance deposited (or dissolved) at an electrode is strictly proportional to the electric charge passed. The conversion chain has only three steps:

Q = I·t　→　 n(e⁻) = Q / F　→　 n(substance) = n(e⁻) / z

Here the Faraday constant F = 96485 C/mol is exactly the total charge of 1 mol of electrons, and z is the number of electrons transferred per ion (z = 2 for Cu²⁺ → Cu). This chain is the metrological backbone of electroplating, the electrolytic refining of copper, and the chlor-alkali industry — in a factory plating bath, "so much charge in, so much copper out", with the accounts settled past the decimal point.

⚠️ Two Big Pitfalls in Electrolysis Calculations
The first is failing to convert time to seconds: the t in I·t must be in seconds (2.0 h = 7200 s); multiplying by hours outright puts you off by three orders of magnitude. The second is forgetting to divide by z: depositing Cu²⁺ takes two electrons, so however large the charge, you still count only half as much copper. Write the electrode reaction before putting pen to paper and circle the z — both pits can then be sidestepped.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: The Quantitative Ledger of Copper Electroplating

Example 2 · Guided Practice (Work With Me)
During the electrolytic refining of copper, a current of 5.0 A passes through the cathode for 2.0 hours. How many grams of copper are deposited in theory? (M(Cu) = 64 g/mol, F = 96485 C/mol)
  1. 💡 Write the cathode reaction first and circle z: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, so z = 2. This step also settles the later "divide by 2".
  2. 💡 Charge Q = I·t = 5.0 A × (2.0 × 3600 s) = 3.6 × 10⁴ C. Convert hours to seconds first — no shortcuts.
  3. 💡 Amount of electrons n(e⁻) = Q / F = 36000 / 96485 ≈ 0.373 mol.
  4. 💡 Convert to copper: n(Cu) = n(e⁻)/z = 0.373/2 ≈ 0.187 mol, m = 0.187 × 64 ≈ 12 g. ✅ Sanity check: 12 g is about the mass of an old coin, plated by a 5 A current over 2 hours — a reasonable order of magnitude. As a handy everyday conversion: 36000 C ≈ 10 Ah, about one-third of the charge a small power bank delivers.

§5 The Lithium-Ion Battery: The Story of a Rocking Chair

In 1991 Sony brought the lithium-ion battery to market, and phones, laptops, and electric vehicles have been sprinting ahead ever since. Its stroke of genius: throughout the whole process no metallic lithium is ever deposited — only Li⁺, the smallest metal ion, shuttles back and forth between two "layered warehouses" — the carbon layers of graphite on one side, the oxide layers of lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO₂) on the other. The model is popularly called the "rocking-chair battery":

Discharge · negative electrode: LixC₆ → xLi⁺ + xe⁻ + 6C　 Discharge · positive electrode: Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ + xLi⁺ + xe⁻ → LiCoO₂

Put this chapter's knowledge in its place: while discharging it is a galvanic cell (a spontaneous reaction, with the high-E LiCoO₂ as the positive electrode), while charging it is an electrolytic cell (the external supply does the work and pushes Li⁺ back against the gradient); the electrolyte and separator play the role of the "salt bridge" — admitting only Li⁺ and blocking electrons. What you carry in your pocket is the most refined heir of the Daniell cell.

Think It Through: In winter a phone drains fast outdoors and may even shut down by itself — which concept from this chapter explains it best?

Hint: Chapter 2 covered the exponential effect of temperature on rate constants; in this chapter the Nernst equation governs the voltage.

Use both threads together: low temperature sends the rates of ion migration and electrode reactions into a dive (the kinetics account, Chapter 2), and the battery-management circuit misreads this as "empty"; meanwhile the internal resistance climbs and the output voltage sags (the Nernst and ohmic-drop account). Back in the warmth, rates and voltage recover and the charge reading often "rises" again — it was never energy frozen away, just energy temporarily locked away.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Redox = electron transfer: a rising oxidation number means electron loss (reducing agent), a falling one means electron gain (oxidizing agent).
  • A galvanic cell splits oxidation and reduction between two electrodes: negative electrode = anode = oxidation; electrons travel from negative to positive through the external circuit, and conduction inside the solution is by ions. The salt bridge keeps both solutions electrically neutral — without it the current stops in an instant.
  • The larger E°, the stronger the oxidized form; the three-step direction test: find the couples → compare E° → E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode > 0 means spontaneous, equivalent to ΔG° = −nFE°.
  • The Nernst equation, qualitatively: oxidized-form concentration ↑ raises E, reduced-form ↑ lowers E; during discharge the voltage keeps sliding down.
  • Electrolysis uses an external supply to drive a non-spontaneous reaction; the anode connects to the positive terminal. Faraday's law: Q = I·t → n(e⁻) = Q/F → n = n(e⁻)/z (F = 96485 C/mol).
  • The lithium-ion battery = a rocking chair: discharge is galvanic, charge is electrolytic; Li⁺ travels between the graphite and LiCoO₂ layers, with no metallic lithium deposited.
