04 Physical Chemistry · Chapter 1

Chemical Thermodynamics: The Steering Wheel of Change

Tear open a disposable hand warmer and it heats itself, never "sucking" heat back from your palm to cool down; crack an ammonium nitrate instant cold pack and it absorbs heat and turns cold — yet it, too, happens all by itself. One releases heat, the other absorbs it, yet both are spontaneous — clearly, "releasing heat" is not the final arbiter of direction. This chapter goes looking for the real judge: first we learn to keep the books of change (internal energy and enthalpy), then to add up paths (Hess's law), and finally we summon ΔG = ΔH − TΔS to settle, once and for all, the question: can this change happen spontaneously?

Introduction: Why the world has a direction

Hot coffee left on the table always cools, yet cold coffee never warms itself; a perfume bottle opened once fills the whole room with fragrance, yet the molecules never politely return to the bottle; a mountain stream runs downhill and never flows back up to the summit. Physics calls changes of this kind — those that happen without external pushing and cannot run themselves backwards — spontaneous processes.

Chemists ask a more concrete question: can a given chemical reaction — iron rusting, glucose oxidation, water freezing — actually happen spontaneously? Under what conditions? This question is valuable because the answer tells us directly: whether a battery can be built, how hot limestone must be fired to become lime, and whether a dream synthesis route is "physically allowed" at all. This chapter proceeds in three steps: first, what exactly we keep accounts of (§1); then two bookkeeping rules (§2, §3); and finally the judge takes the bench (§4).

Think It Through: opening a perfume bottle, the molecules soon spread through the whole room; they never return to the bottle on their own. Is the "energy" difference between these two situations large? Where does the real difference lie?

Hint: compare the two arrangements — "all molecules in the bottle" versus "molecules spread evenly through the room". Which one can be realized in more ways?

Energetically the two are almost identical — the kinetic energy of the flying molecules has not changed. The real difference is the number of possible arrangements: "spread through the room" can be realized in countless ways, while "all back in the bottle" allows only a handful. The world naturally favors states that can be realized in more ways — that is the entropy that §4 will quantify. The sense of direction often comes not from energy but from "disorder".

§1 Systems, surroundings, and state functions

Before keeping accounts, draw the ledger's boundary. The portion of matter under study is the system; everything outside it is the surroundings. If you study hot water in a cup, the water is the system while the cup, the tabletop, and the air are the surroundings. In the laboratory, a reaction in an open beaker can exchange both matter and energy with its surroundings, while a sealed bomb calorimeter exchanges only energy. Thermodynamics cares about how much heat and work are exchanged between the two.

The system's "net worth" is described by quantities that depend only on its current state: temperature, pressure, volume, and the internal energy U. Such quantities are called state functions — they depend only on the starting point and the end point, not on the path taken. Heat Q and work W, by contrast, are path quantities: they describe "how the journey was made", and the same trip done differently yields different values.

Think It Through: returning from the mountaintop lodge to the parking lot, by cable car or on foot. Which of "drop in elevation" and "energy you expended" behaves like a state function?

Hint: change the way you descend — which number never changes?

The drop in elevation depends only on the start and end points — by cable car or by trekking over ridges it is 800 m either way, so it is a "state function". The energy you expend depends entirely on the length and style of the route — it is a "path quantity". In thermodynamics: ΔU is like the elevation difference; Q and W are like the effort — which explains why the same reaction can release different amounts of heat in different apparatus, while the change in internal energy stays the same.

📌 The key idea in one sentence
State functions are like elevation (only the endpoints matter); path quantities are like the effort of the walk (the route matters). The protagonists of this chapter — U, H, S, G — are all state functions, which is why their differences ΔH and ΔG can be freely split apart and recombined as in §3.

§2 The first law of thermodynamics and enthalpy

Written into the chemist's ledger, conservation of energy becomes the first law of thermodynamics:

ΔU = Q + W

Using the common sign convention: Q > 0 the system absorbs heat, Q < 0 the system releases heat; W > 0 the surroundings do work on the system, W < 0 the system does work on the surroundings. Example: a gas absorbs 50 J of heat while expanding and doing 20 J of work on a piston, so W = −20 J and ΔU = 50 + (−20) = +30 J — part of the heat taken in was spent as work done on the outside, and the rest was deposited into internal energy. The law says: energy is never created or destroyed, only transferred between system and surroundings; a perpetual-motion machine cannot be built.

Enthalpy: an energy account tailored for open vessels

Laboratory reactions are almost always run at constant pressure (open vessels under an unchanging atmosphere). Whenever gas is produced or consumed, the system must do some "expansion work" pushing the atmosphere back, an item ΔU does not settle cleanly. So thermodynamics defines a new state function — enthalpy, H = U + pV — which exactly covers that work and makes the heat budget of a constant-pressure process neat and tidy:

at constant pressure Qp = ΔH

That is why chemists talk about ΔH from dawn to dusk: the "heat of reaction" measured at constant pressure is precisely the enthalpy change. The signs tell the story at a glance:

SignMeaningSystem's energyFeels likeExamples (per mole)
ΔH < 0Exothermic reactionEnthalpy drops; surplus heat flows to the surroundingswarmerMethane combustion −890 kJ; neutralization −57.3 kJ
ΔH > 0Endothermic reactionEnthalpy rises; heat is "borrowed" from the surroundingscoolerIce melting +6.0 kJ; ammonium nitrate dissolving in water +25.8 kJ

Note the hidden landmine in the "feels like" column: exothermic ≠ spontaneous, and endothermic ≠ non-spontaneous — the endothermic dissolution in the last row, which happens all by itself, is precisely the open case that §4 will resolve.

§3 Hess's law: adding and subtracting heats of reaction

Hess's law states that the reaction enthalpy depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path taken. When only pressure-volume work occurs, qp = ΔH at constant pressure and qv = ΔU at constant volume; use consistent conditions when combining data. This is Hess's law. It is really a direct corollary of the fact that ΔH is the increment of a state function — since only the endpoints matter, ΔH values add and subtract exactly as the equations do, like the altitude gained on a mountain trek accumulated stage by stage.

Its greatest use is to calculate reaction heats that cannot be measured. Some reactions cannot be cleanly isolated for calorimetry: incomplete carbon combustion often produces both CO and CO₂. Hess's law lets us calculate the enthalpy of forming CO from other measured reactions:

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: find ΔH for "C burned to CO"

Example 1 · Full demonstration (watch me do it)
Given ① C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₁ = −393.5 kJ/mol; ② CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH₂ = −283.0 kJ/mol. Find ΔH for C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g).
  1. Recognize the difficulty: carbon burning in oxygen cannot be stopped at the CO stage (the CO formed is immediately oxidized further), so the target reaction's ΔH cannot be measured directly — exactly the signal for Hess's law to step in.
  2. Design the route: target = ① − ②. Check the matter account: ①'s C + O₂ + CO₂ minus ②'s CO + ½O₂ + CO₂ — CO₂ cancels on both sides, O₂ leaves 1 − ½ = ½, giving C + ½O₂ → CO ✓ exactly the target.
  3. Do the same arithmetic on ΔH: ΔH = ΔH₁ − ΔH₂ = (−393.5) − (−283.0) = −110.5 kJ/mol. Note that "subtracting a negative number" means adding its absolute value — don't drop the sign.
  4. Sanity check: C → CO₂ releases 393.5 kJ, of which C → CO releases 110.5 and CO → CO₂ releases 283.0; 110.5 + 283.0 = 393.5 ✓ the two routes' "height differences" close perfectly. Answer: ΔH = −110.5 kJ/mol (exothermic).

§4 Entropy and Gibbs free energy: the final judge of direction

Entropy: the world's other preference

A brand-new deck of cards comes sorted neatly by suit; shuffle a few times and it is chaos. Drop it on the floor and it will never re-stack itself. The molecular world is the same: in ice's crystal lattice every water molecule "stands in formation"; once melted into water each wanders freely and the number of arrangements explodes. Thermodynamics uses entropy to measure this "number of ways of being realized" — intuitively, entropy is a measure of the system's disorder (more strictly: of the number of microstates): the larger S, the more possible microscopic arrangements. Gas expanding, a substance dissolving, a crystal melting — in all of them entropy increases.

ΔG: two judges merged into one

Now put both driving forces into a single formula. In the 1870s Gibbs defined the Gibbs free energy and proved that at constant temperature and pressure:

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

ΔH is the energy term (negative when heat is released, pushing the reaction forward); TΔS is the entropy term (positive when entropy grows, and the higher the temperature the harder it pushes). The two play tug-of-war, and the net push is ΔG:

📌 The spontaneity criterion (constant T and p)
ΔG < 0 the forward reaction is spontaneous; ΔG = 0 the system is at equilibrium; ΔG > 0 the forward reaction is not spontaneous (the reverse reaction is). The ice-melting case is thereby solved: ΔH = +6.0 kJ/mol "objects", but at 298 K the term TΔS ≈ 6.6 kJ/mol "pushes", so ΔG = 6.0 − 6.6 = −0.6 kJ/mol < 0 — entropy wins, so an endothermic change still proceeds spontaneously.

Lay out the four sign combinations of ΔH and ΔS in one table, and the role of temperature becomes plain:

🪜 Three-Level Ladder · Why isn't "lowest energy" enough to guarantee spontaneity?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

The world has more than one preference: besides "flow downhill" there is "shuffle towards disorder". Waterfalls race to lower ground (energy decreasing) and perfume spreads through the room (disorder increasing) — the two currents often run the same way, fooling us into thinking "lowest energy = always spontaneous". Ice melting at room temperature runs the other way: it absorbs heat and its energy rises, but its molecules gain freedom from the tidy lattice — disorder's winning margin outweighs energy's loss. Watch only the energy and you have read only half the map.

LEVEL 2 · Data

Settle it with the formula. Ice melting: ΔH = +6.0 kJ/mol, ΔS = +22 J/(mol·K) = +0.022 kJ/(mol·K). At 298 K (25 °C), ΔG = 6.0 − 298×0.022 = 6.0 − 6.6 = −0.6 kJ/mol, spontaneous; at 268 K (−5 °C), ΔG = 6.0 − 5.9 = +0.1 kJ/mol, not spontaneous — the ice stays solid. The crossover sits exactly at T* = ΔH/ΔS = 6000/22 ≈ 273 K — that is 0 °C. Same reaction, temperature changes the verdict: a phenomenon "lowest energy" cannot explain, but ΔG can.

LEVEL 3 · The essence

The real judge is the second law of thermodynamics: the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. When the system absorbs heat ΔH on melting, the surroundings lose that heat, so the surroundings' entropy change is ΔSsurr = −ΔH/T (negative: the surroundings release heat to the system). The universe's total entropy change is therefore ΔStotal = ΔS − ΔH/T. Multiply both sides by −T: −TΔStotal = ΔH − TΔS = ΔG. So ΔG < 0 ⇔ ΔStotal > 0 — ΔG is merely a way of folding "the surroundings' half of the ledger" into the system's books, and its verdict is always signed: the total entropy of the universe increases.

⚠️ The unit trap (the #1 score-killer in this chapter)
ΔH is usually quoted in kJ/mol, while ΔS is usually quoted in J/(mol·K). Before substituting into ΔG = ΔH − TΔS you must unify them: divide ΔS by 1000. Multiply 160.5 J straight by 298 K and TΔS comes out a thousand times too large — "not spontaneous" flips to "spontaneous" in an instant. One unit, a reversed verdict. Always convert first, before anything else.
⚠️ Misconception Buster · Exothermic vs. spontaneous
✗
Misconception To judge whether a reaction can happen spontaneously, just look at whether it releases or absorbs heat: exothermic reactions can, endothermic ones cannot.
✓
Correct Looking only at ΔH is seeing only half the picture. Ice melting (ΔH = +6.0 kJ/mol) and ammonium nitrate dissolving in water (ΔH = +25.8 kJ/mol — the test tube feels ice-cold) are both endothermic yet spontaneous, because the entropy term TΔS is larger; conversely, an exothermic reaction with a steep entropy decrease (ammonia synthesis at very high temperature, say) can turn ΔG positive. The only judge is whether ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative.

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: use ΔG to judge whether a decomposition can happen

Example 2 · Guided practice (do it with me)
Calcining limestone: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g), ΔH = +178.2 kJ/mol, ΔS = +160.5 J/(mol·K). (1) Is it spontaneous at 300 K? (2) Is it spontaneous at 1200 K? (3) Find the crossover temperature, and explain why lime kilns must be fired to about 900 °C. (Each step gives a hint first — work it out yourself, then expand to compare.)
  1. 💡 Step one is always lining up the units: ΔS = 160.5 J/(mol·K) ÷ 1000 = 0.1605 kJ/(mol·K). Leave ΔH in kJ/mol, untouched.
  2. 💡 At 300 K: ΔG = 178.2 − 300×0.1605 = 178.2 − 48.2 = +130.0 kJ/mol > 0, not spontaneous at ordinary temperatures — which is why limestone on mountainsides has stayed undecomposed for eons.
  3. 💡 At 1200 K: ΔG = 178.2 − 1200×0.1605 = 178.2 − 192.6 = −14.4 kJ/mol < 0, spontaneous at high temperature. Note ΔS > 0 (a gas is produced), so the TΔS term climbs ever faster with temperature until it finally overtakes.
  4. 💡 Crossover temperature T* = ΔH/ΔS = 178.2/0.1605 ≈ 1110 K (about 837 °C). Firing a lime kiln above 900 °C is exactly to clear this threshold with margin to spare. ✅ Self-check: T* lies between 300 K and 1200 K, consistent with (1) not spontaneous and (2) spontaneous.
Think It Through: does ΔG = 0 mean the reaction has "stopped dead"?

Hint: recall how the forward and reverse rates relate at chemical equilibrium.

Nothing "stops". ΔG = 0 is equilibrium: the forward and reverse reactions still proceed every instant, just at equal rates, so the macroscopic concentrations no longer change. ΔG is like the slope of a hill: farther from equilibrium the slope is steeper (the driving force stronger); at the valley floor the slope is zero — but the molecular coming and going never ceases. This also reminds us: ΔG governs "which way and how far", never "how fast".

⚠️ Misconception Buster · Negative ΔG and everything's fine?
✗
Misconception As long as ΔG < 0 works out, the reaction will proceed smoothly — and quickly, too.
✓
Correct Thermodynamics answers only "can it, and which way" — never "how fast". Diamond turning to graphite has ΔG < 0, yet the ring on your finger is perfectly safe — the rate is measured in eons. Rate is the business of kinetics, the star of the next chapter; many industrial reactions with strongly negative ΔG still rely on high temperature and catalysts precisely to satisfy both chapters at once.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Draw the system/surroundings boundary first, then keep accounts: ΔU, H, S, G are all state functions (endpoints only); Q and W are path quantities.
  • First law ΔU = Q + W: energy is conserved; perpetual motion is impossible.
  • The heat of reaction at constant pressure is the enthalpy change: ΔH < 0 exothermic, ΔH > 0 endothermic.
  • Hess's law: ΔH values add and subtract as the equations do — unmeasurable heats of reaction can be computed "by detour".
  • Entropy S measures disorder; the sole judge of spontaneity is ΔG = ΔH − TΔS (ΔG<0 spontaneous, =0 equilibrium, >0 reverse spontaneous).
  • Of the four ΔH/ΔS combinations, the two same-sign cases have a crossover temperature T* = ΔH/ΔS; always unify units before computing (divide ΔS by 1000).
