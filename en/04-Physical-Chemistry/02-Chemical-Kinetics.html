04 Physical Chemistry · Chapter 2

Chemical Kinetics: The Stopwatch of Change

The same bottle of fresh milk turns sour in a day on the counter, keeps for a week in the fridge, and can even last for months in the freezer. Thermodynamics long ago passed the death sentence on "spoilage" (ΔG < 0), yet it never says when the sentence is carried out — the previous chapter governed direction; this one governs time. Chemical kinetics studies how fast reactions go and by what path: How is a rate defined and measured? By what factors do concentration, temperature, and catalysts each accelerate it? Why does a mere 10 °C of warming double the rate? The answer hides in the "long tail" of the molecular energy distribution.

Introduction: Speed Is Chemistry Too

An iron nail rusts over days, a steel plant melts a heat of steel in tens of minutes, and an airbag inflates fully within 30 milliseconds of a crash — all of them are oxidation reactions, yet their speeds differ by sixteen orders of magnitude. What decides the difference is not "should it happen" (thermodynamics' turf) but "how the molecules meet one another and how they cross the threshold" (kinetics' turf).

The two questions look parallel but are in fact complementary: thermodynamics says "only the downhill road is open", while kinetics says "coming down the mountain still means crossing a pass — how high the pass is and how many get over it decide when you reach the foot of the mountain". This chapter unfolds in the order "first measure the speed (§1) → find the pattern (§2) → explain temperature (§3) → bring in the catalyst (§4)", and at the end you will understand why the refrigerator, the match, the catalytic converter, and the enzyme are, at bottom, four disguises of one and the same thing.

§1 Rate of Reaction: Clocking Change

A rate is simply "how fast the concentration changes". For reactants we take how fast they are consumed, for products how fast they accumulate — always as a positive value:

v = |Δc / Δt|, most commonly in units of mol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹

Take ammonia synthesis: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. Every 1 mol of N₂ consumed necessarily comes with 3 mol of H₂ consumed and 2 mol of NH₃ formed, so the speeds the three species "report" are not the same — H₂ is consumed three times as fast as N₂, and NH₃ is formed twice as fast as N₂. To put everyone on the same scale, we agree to divide by the respective stoichiometric coefficients: v(N₂)/1 = v(H₂)/3 = v(NH₃)/2, so that the rate has the same value whichever species is used to express it.

How do we "see" a rate experimentally? Any property that varies monotonically with concentration can serve as the stopwatch: pressure (reactions with a change in the number of gas moles), color (e.g. the fading of KMnO₄), electrical conductivity (changing ion concentrations), volume (a gas burette). The essence of a kinetics experiment is exactly this: plot the "concentration–time" curve, and its slope is the rate.

Think It Through: For the reaction 2N₂O₅ → 4NO₂ + O₂, which is larger — the rate expressed through the consumption of N₂O₅, or the rate expressed through the formation of O₂? By how many times?

Hint: one O₂ is "born" only for every two N₂O₅ that "disappear".

The rate of formation of O₂ is 1/2 of the rate of consumption of N₂O₅ (or, put the other way, the N₂O₅ rate is twice the O₂ rate). In the same reaction at the same instant, the rates of different species can differ several-fold — so a reported rate must always state "expressed in terms of which species", or be converted to a single scale via the stoichiometric coefficients.

§2 The Rate Equation and the Order of Reaction: The Pattern Comes from Experiment

Writing the relationship between rate and concentration as a mathematical expression gives the rate equation. For a common reaction:

v = k[A]m[B]n

where k is the rate constant — it changes as soon as the temperature or the catalyst changes; the exponents m and n are the orders of reaction with respect to A and B, and m + n is the overall order. Zeroth order (concentration does not affect the rate), first order, second order, order 1.5 … all genuinely exist.

The most crucial rule: orders are determined by experiment and must never be read off the coefficients of the equation. Most reactions do not complete in a single step but go through several (see §4), and the rate is set by the slowest of them, which often fails to match the overall equation's coefficients. Classic counterexamples: the experimental rate equation for H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr is complicated enough to carry fractional powers and ratio terms, while the superficially "simple" NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂ is measured at low temperature to follow v = k[NO₂]² — flatly independent of [CO].

⚠️ Copying Orders from Coefficients = Giving Away Free Marks
Seeing aA + bB → products and writing v = k[A]a[B]b is the most classic of mistakes. The only exception is an elementary reaction — a reaction completed in a single step — where the coefficients may be used (this is the law of mass action). Whether an overall reaction qualifies is decided by experiment alone.

  1. 💡 Pick a pair to compare: compare 1 with 2 — [B] is held fixed while [A] doubles (0.10→0.20), and the rate goes from 0.010 to 0.040, a 4-fold change. 2m = 4 → m = 2.
  2. 💡 Now compare 2 with 3 — this time [A] is fixed and [B] becomes 3 times as large (0.10→0.30), and the rate goes from 0.040 to 0.120, exactly 3-fold. 3n = 3 → n = 1.

Orders are always decided by the data.
  4. 💡 Substitute the data of experiment 1 to find k: 0.010 = k × (0.10)² × 0.10 = k × 0.0010 → k = 10 L²·mol⁻²·s⁻¹. ✅ Self-check: verify with experiment 3 — 10×(0.20)²×0.30 = 10×0.012 = 0.120 ✓, matching the data.
Think It Through: The experimental rate equation for the decomposition of H₂O₂ is v = k[H₂O₂], unrelated to the coefficient 2 in "2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂". What does this hint at?

Hint: what if the step that sets the pace requires only one H₂O₂ molecule…

The rate depends only on the first power of [H₂O₂], which means the rate-determining step (see §4) involves just one H₂O₂ molecule, not two H₂O₂ colliding head-on. In fact the reaction proceeds in several steps, with intermediates such as I⁻ acting as go-betweens. A rate equation is a clue in a detective story: read backwards, it helps chemists reconstruct the reaction mechanism.

§3 Temperature and Activation Energy: The Exponential Speedometer

Collision Theory: What Kind of Collision Counts as "Effective"

The first act of a reaction is molecules colliding. But the molecules collide billions of billions of times every second, and after the overwhelming majority they simply part ways — collision theory says that only an effective collision satisfying two conditions at once can set a reaction going:

  • Enough energy: the relative kinetic energy must clear a certain threshold, and this threshold — the difference between the minimum energy of an activated molecule and the average energy of the reactants — is the activation energy, Ea;
  • Correct orientation: the molecules must meet at the proper angle — if the reactive sites fail to line up, even abundant energy is wasted on the collision.

The Arrhenius Equation: The Threshold Written into an Exponent

In 1889, Arrhenius condensed the picture above into a formula:

k = A·e−Ea/RT

A is the "frequency factor" (how often collisions happen and what fraction have the right orientation), and the exponential term e−Ea/RT is the "fraction with enough energy". Ea sits in the numerator of the exponent while T sits inside the product in the denominator — this is the mathematical root of why a tiny change in temperature turns the rate upside down. Rule of thumb: near room temperature, every 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate.

This curve is called the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, the hidden protagonist behind all of this chapter's "temperature magic".

🪜 A Ladder of Three Rungs · Warm by 10 °C and the rate doubles — the temperature rose a mere 3%, so what justifies that?
LEVEL 1 · Analogy

Imagine a class with a mean score of 60 and a pass line of 90. On the final exam the mean rises by just 3 points (up 5%), yet the number of students passing may double — because the "borderline students" crowded at 88 and 89 are the densest group, and a small rightward shift of the mean pushes a whole batch of them over the line. Molecular energy works the same way: the mean kinetic energy rises only 3% with temperature, yet the molecules piled up just below the activation-energy line are precisely the densest crowd, so a tiny rightward shift of the distribution sends molecules over the line in droves.

LEVEL 2 · Numbers

Mean kinetic energy ∝ T: 298 K → 308 K is a rise of only 3.4%. But the rate constant answers to the Arrhenius equation. Take Ea = 50 kJ/mol (a typical magnitude) and R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹: on warming, e−Ea/RT grows by a factor of eEₐ/R × (1/298 − 1/308); the exponent equals 50000/8.314 × 10/(298×308) ≈ 0.655, and e0.655 ≈ 1.9 times — precisely where the "doubling" rule of thumb comes from. The temperature change is amplified by the exponent: 3% goes in, 100% comes out.

LEVEL 3 · The Essence

The Boltzmann factor e−Eₐ/RT comes from statistical mechanics: the fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea decays exponentially in energy. The tail of the distribution curve is a long exponential tail — the head (the low-energy region) is crowded with molecules while the tail is sparsely populated; as temperature shifts the distribution rightward, the head contributes little while the tail's share explodes. This is also why reactions with a larger Ea are more temperature-sensitive (the doubling rule is most accurate near Ea ≈ 50 kJ/mol; the actual factor ranges from about 1.2 to 4, and "doubling" is only an empirical average near room temperature).

Think It Through: A woodpile will not burn on its own, yet striking one match can set the whole pile alight. What exactly does the fire-lighter provide?

Hint: what "admission fee" does the match's flame pay on behalf of the molecules at the wood's surface?

What it provides is the admission fee of activation energy. The flame pushes a local batch of molecules over the barrier; a little wood burns first, and the heat released pushes more molecules over the line — the reaction pays its own "ignition fee", and once lit it sustains itself. An engine's spark plug, a lighter's flint, and the barrier lowered by protein-digesting enzymes all play the same role: advancing Ea up front. How much net heat can be released once the fire is burning is ΔH's business (previous chapter).

📐 Worked-Example Scaffold: Mental Arithmetic with the "Doubling Rule"

Example 2 · Full Demonstration (watch me do it)
A reaction takes 8 hours to complete at 20 °C. About how long would it take at 50 °C? (Do the arithmetic in your head with the rule of thumb "every 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate".)
  1. Identify the tool: the rule of thumb is a relation about rates, while the problem gives a time. Time is inversely proportional to rate (for the same "amount of reaction", a larger v means a smaller t), so first compute the rate factor, then take its reciprocal.
  2. Count the steps: 50 − 20 = 30 °C = three "10 °C steps", so the rate increases by about 2³ = 8 times.
  3. Convert to time: rate ×8 → time ÷8: 8 hours ÷ 8 = 1 hour.
  4. ✅ Self-check and limits: the mental arithmetic is reasonable (8 h → 1 h). Note that this is a rough estimate — if the reaction's Ea departs from 50 kJ/mol, the factor departs from 2; at high temperature, side reactions and decomposition come to meddle as well. Use the rule for "order-of-magnitude judgment", never for precise calculation.

§4 Catalysts and Reaction Mechanisms: The Art of the Shortcut

Catalysts: A New Route, Not a New Destination

A catalyst can speed up a reaction enormously, and its secret fits in one sentence: it takes part in the reaction, provides a new pathway of lower activation energy, and is regenerated unscathed at the end. Lower the threshold from 80 kJ/mol to 45 kJ/mol and, by the Arrhenius calculation, the rate at 298 K can rise by a factor on the order of a million — while the initial and final states of the reaction have not moved one bit.

📌 The Eternal Identity on the Energy-Level Diagram
The difference between the activation energy of the forward reaction and that of the reverse reaction is the reaction heat: ΔH = Ea(forward) − Ea(reverse). A catalyst lowers the two Ea values by equal amounts, so ΔH is unchanged, ΔG is unchanged, and the equilibrium constant and the equilibrium position are all unchanged — a catalyst only helps you reach equilibrium sooner, not reach an equilibrium further away.

⚠️ Misconception Buster · The Two Masks of a Catalyst
✗
Misconception 1 "A catalyst does not take part in the reaction; it just stands by and watches."
✓
The truth A catalyst participates deeply: it forms intermediates with the reactants and reroutes the reaction (the green winding road in the diagram above); it is simply regenerated in full in later steps, which is why macroscopically it "never appears in the equation, and its amount never dwindles". Participating yet unconsumed — the two are not contradictory.
✗
Misconception 2 "Adding a catalyst raises the yield."
✓
The truth A catalyst changes only Eₐ and k, not ΔH, ΔG, or the equilibrium constant K; the equilibrium conversion does not budge — equilibrium is simply reached sooner. To raise the yield you must move the temperature, pressure, or concentrations — that is the territory of ΔG and equilibrium from the previous chapter.

Reaction Mechanisms and the Rate-Determining Step: The Slowest Station on the Line

The overall equation is only the "headline balance"; the step-by-step ledger at the molecular level is called the reaction mechanism. For example, NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂ (at low temperature) proceeds in two steps:

① NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO (slow) ② NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂ (fast)

The overall rate is set by the slowest step — just as the output of an assembly line is capped by its slowest station; that step is called the rate-determining step. In the mechanism above the slow step contains only two NO₂, so experimentally v = k[NO₂]², independent of [CO] — the mystery from §2 of "orders do not follow the coefficients" is solved here: the exponents of the rate equation reveal the face of the rate-determining step. Enzymes are nature's most ingenious catalysts precisely because they rewrite the rate-determining steps of reactions that would otherwise occur once in tens of millions of years (such as nitrogen fixation) down to the millisecond scale.

🧺 Chapter Summary

  • Rate v = |Δc/Δt|; when the same reaction is expressed with different species, convert to a single scale via the stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Rate equation v = k[A]m[B]n: the orders are determined by experiment; only an elementary reaction may have them written from the coefficients.
  • An effective collision = sufficient energy (≥ Eₐ) + correct orientation; Eₐ is the energy barrier and is a different thing from ΔH.
  • Arrhenius k = A·e−Eₐ/RT: temperature sits in the exponent, and a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate — the root cause is the exploding share of the energy distribution's tail.
  • A catalyst participates in the reaction, lowers Eₐ, provides a new pathway, and is regenerated at the end; it changes neither ΔH, ΔG, nor the equilibrium, and it catalyzes forward and reverse alike.
  • An overall reaction proceeds in steps, with the rate set by the rate-determining step; read backwards, the rate equation is evidence for inferring the mechanism.
